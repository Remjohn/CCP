# **The Psycholinguistics of Emotion and Discourse Markers Under Emotional Load: The Expression Signature and Voice DNA Bridge**

## **Introduction to the Expression Signature and Voice DNA Bridge**

The translation of human emotion into spoken language is a profound neurobiological, cognitive, and structural event that radically alters the architecture of discourse. When an individual—such as a coach, mentor, or leader—transitions from a state of calm, analytical explanation to a state of high-arousal righteous indignation, the downstream effects on their language production are immediate, measurable, and highly predictable. This phenomenon forms the foundational scientific bedrock for the Expression Signature and the Voice DNA Bridge. Rather than treating an individual's speaking style as an arbitrary collection of aesthetic choices or rhetorical habits, contemporary psycholinguistics, pragmatics, and affective neuroscience collectively reveal that syntactic structures, discourse markers, and function word distributions are the direct, causal outputs of specific emotional architectures and cognitive states.

This comprehensive research report addresses a critical diagnostic inquiry: When a coach's emotional state shifts abruptly toward peak conviction or righteous indignation, what specifically occurs within their grammatical framework? Which discourse markers emerge, which vanish, and which migrate to new syntactic positions? Furthermore, how does the depth of subordinate clauses change under the intense cognitive demands of high arousal, and why do sentences inevitably compress? By answering these questions through the lens of empirical data, this analysis provides the extraction methodology of the 12-step pipeline with a rigorous scientific basis, transitioning the system from an observed pattern basis to a validated diagnostic instrument.

The literature synthesizing these mechanics is anchored by four foundational pillars. First, James Pennebaker’s longitudinal research on the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) system establishes that subconscious function words—not semantic content words—are the true biometric indicators of identity and emotional load. Second, Deborah Schiffrin’s landmark theoretical frameworks regarding discourse markers delineate the pragmatic, multi-planar connective tissue of speech, explaining positional shifts under cognitive duress. Third, the affective neuroscience and experimental psychology of Elizabeth Kensinger, Jeanette Altarriba, and Lisa Bauer document the arousal-syntax relationship, proving that sentence compression and imperative frequency spikes are biological mandates, not stylistic preferences. Finally, the recent computational linguistics advancements (2020–2025) led by Verónica Pérez-Rosas translate these theoretical constructs into automated, RoBERTa-based extraction models, defining the diagnostic features and failure modes of real-time emotion classification. Together, these domains establish the causal chain that explains why specific emotional architectures produce specific linguistic construction patterns, thereby validating the discourse marker census and the rhythm signature variable at the core of the Voice DNA system.

## **The Subconscious Lexicon: Function Word Distribution and Emotional Identity**

The fundamental premise of the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) framework, developed extensively by James Pennebaker from its inception in the early 1990s through the publication of *The Secret Life of Pronouns* (2011) and the recent LIWC-22 iterations, is a paradigm shift in text analysis. The most psychologically revealing words in the English language are simultaneously the most forgettable and the least consciously controlled. While content words—comprising nouns, regular verbs, and adjectives—convey the explicit semantic meaning and topical focus of a statement, function words serve as the cognitive cement that binds the narrative together.1 Function words, which include pronouns, prepositions, articles, conjunctions, and auxiliary verbs, constitute less than 0.04 percent of the average English vocabulary.1 Yet, they account for more than half of the words utilized in daily human speech.1 Because function words are processed rapidly and subconsciously in different regions of the brain than content words, they operate independently of the speaker's conscious rhetorical filtering, serving as highly reliable, unfiltered biometric indicators of internal psychological states, social intelligence, and identity.3

### **The Attentional Spotlight: Pronoun Shifts and Emotional Load**

Pronouns function as a highly sensitive attentional spotlight, revealing the exact coordinates where a speaker is directing their cognitive and emotional focus at any given millisecond. The fluctuation of this spotlight is intrinsically diagnostic of shifting emotional registers. Decades of LIWC analysis reveal that baseline states of clinical depression, trauma, severe anxiety, and acute psychological distress are heavily correlated with a deeply inward-focused attentional spotlight. Linguistically, this manifests as a highly elevated rate of first-person singular pronouns (*I, me, my, mine, myself*).1 This pattern reflects a neurocognitive state of rumination, self-preoccupation, and a psychological isolation from the surrounding environment.6

However, when the emotional load of a coach or speaker shifts from calm instruction to anger—and specifically to righteous indignation—the pronoun distribution undergoes a radical structural inversion. Righteous indignation is defined psycholinguistically and behaviorally as a variant of anger associated with moral violations, shock, and a profound sense of betrayal regarding established norms.7 Unlike baseline frustration, which arises simply when a goal is blocked, righteous indignation forces the attentional spotlight violently outward. The empirical data indicates that during states of indignation, the use of first-person singular pronouns drops significantly. In its place, the discourse experiences a dramatic surge in second-person pronouns (*you, your*) and third-person plural pronouns (*they, them*).6

This pronoun shift is not a stylistic or rhetorical choice; it is a cognitive necessity dictated by the emotional architecture of the moment. To successfully express righteous indignation, the speaker must psychologically project blame, establish a definitive moral boundary, and actively engage with the source of the frustration or the audience witnessing the violation.7 The high frequency of *you* and *they* signals an outward focus, an immediate engagement with the external world, and an active assessment of the righteousness of the target's actions.6

Furthermore, the integration of first-person plural pronouns (*we, us, our*) serves as a powerful metric of social cohesion, hierarchical dominance, and leadership under pressure. High-status individuals, self-confident leaders, and coaches exerting moral authority consistently exhibit lower frequencies of "I-words" and significantly higher frequencies of "we-words," signaling a focus on the collective and a secure psychological distance from the isolated self.4 During moments of peak conviction, an abrupt shift from *I* to *we* indicates that the speaker is attempting to forcibly align the listener's moral framework with their own. This pronoun swap establishes a shared participation framework based on mutual indignation or shared values, essentially conscripting the listener into the speaker's emotional state.9

### **Analytical Thinking, Cognitive Complexity, and Exclusive Words**

Beyond the attentional spotlight of pronouns, the distribution of articles, prepositions, and exclusive words provides a direct, quantifiable window into the cognitive complexity and analytical depth of the speaker. The LIWC-22 system introduced the Analytical Thinking summary measure, originally published as the Categorical-Dynamic Index (CDI), to capture the degree to which a speaker utilizes formal, logical, and hierarchical thinking patterns.10

When a coach is operating in a calm, instructional, and analytical state, their language typically scores very high in analytical thinking. This high-CDI state is characterized linguistically by a high density of nouns, articles (*a, an, the*), and prepositions (*in, on, under, above*). This distribution indicates a cognitive focus on rigid categorization, object permanence, and the spatial or logical relationships between concrete entities.6 The speaker is constructing a complex, hierarchical mental model for the listener.

However, as emotional arousal increases and the speaker transitions into righteous indignation, the cognitive load required to maintain these hierarchical, categorical sentence structures becomes neurologically unsustainable. Consequently, the speaker's language becomes less analytical and significantly more dynamic. This dynamic state is marked by an increase in active verbs, adverbs, and pronouns, functioning alongside a sharp decrease in complex prepositional phrases.10 The discourse transitions from a stable description of how things are to an active, dynamic assertion of what must be done or what has been violated.

The presence of "exclusive words" (*but, except, without, exclude*) further nuances the measurement of cognitive complexity under emotional load. Exclusive words demand high cognitive overhead; they require the speaker to make complex cognitive distinctions by first acknowledging a category and then actively removing an element from it.1 A high frequency of exclusive words, combined with specific pronoun configurations, is strongly correlated with cognitive complexity, aging, and psychological distancing.11 Furthermore, exclusive words are highly diagnostic of honesty and truth-telling. Truth-tellers must meticulously delineate what actually happened from what did not happen, whereas deceptive individuals tend to avoid exclusive words to keep their narratives vague and limit cognitive strain.1 In a state of righteous indignation, the preservation or sudden spike of exclusive words indicates that the coach is establishing rigid, non-negotiable moral boundaries, defining exactly what behaviors are excluded from acceptable norms.

## **Pragmatic Connective Tissue: Discourse Markers Under Cognitive Load**

If LIWC's function word distribution represents the subconscious psychological state of the speaker, Deborah Schiffrin’s foundational work on discourse markers represents the real-time, pragmatic management of the human interaction. Discourse markers—such as the particles *oh, well, now, then, you know, I mean*, and the connectives *so, because, and, but, or*—are defined as relatively syntax-independent linguistic elements that manage the flow and structure of discourse without changing the truth-conditional meaning of the sentence.12 Rather than functioning as meaningless fillers, these markers provide vital contextual coordinates that guide the listener in interpreting the speaker's intent, managing the flow of information, and maintaining interactional coherence.13

### **The Multi-Planar Functions of Discourse Connectives**

Schiffrin’s 1987 landmark research, integrated with three decades of subsequent pragmatics literature, establishes that discourse markers operate simultaneously across multiple, intersecting planes of talk.15 This multidimensionality is the scientific explanation for the CCF's observation regarding positional shifts: it explains why the exact same marker appears in radically different syntactic positions depending on the emotional and cognitive load of the speaker. The planes of discourse include:

1. **The Ideational Structure**: The explicit semantic relationship between propositions (e.g., establishing a direct cause and effect between two real-world events).  
2. **The Action Structure**: The sequence of speech acts being performed (e.g., offering a justification immediately following a contentious claim).  
3. **The Exchange Structure**: The mechanics of turn-taking, floor-holding, and speaker transition.  
4. **The Participation Framework**: The interpersonal relationship between the speaker, the listener, and the utterance itself (e.g., displaying alignment, non-alignment, or deference).  
5. **The Information State**: The cognitive management of knowledge, attention, and memory retrieval.

Consider the structural connective marker *but*. In a calm, analytical state, *but* frequently operates primarily on the ideational plane, simply contrasting two factual propositions within a single clause. However, when a coach shifts into a state of high emotional load or indignation, the function of *but* radically expands across the planes. An utterance beginning with *but* during an emotional exchange may simultaneously preface an idea unit (ideational), display a non-aligned participation framework by rejecting the premise of the opponent (participation), realize a forceful rebuttal in the action structure (action), and aggressively seize the floor in the exchange structure.15 Under emotional load, the marker becomes deeply saturated with interpersonal and cognitive significance.

### **Cognitive Load, Emotional Activation, and Positional Shifts**

The transition into high-arousal emotional states inherently increases the cognitive load on the speaker. Working memory must simultaneously process the emotional trigger, formulate a syntactically coherent response, and manage the highly volatile social dynamics of the interaction. Under this extreme pressure, discourse markers act as both cognitive release valves and structural scaffolding, shifting their physical position in the sentence based on Talk Time (TTT) transitions.

For instance, the cognitive marker *oh* is primarily used to signal a shift in the speaker's information state, indicating a sudden realization or the receipt of new knowledge.16 Under high emotional activation, *oh* frequently migrates to the absolute initial position of an intonation unit. This structural placement—occurring exactly at the point of speaker change or immediately following a prior intonation contour—classifies it as a prototypical discourse marker.17 The sudden realization indexed by an initial-position *oh* allows the speaker to pause, mentally recalibrate their cognitive framework, and manage the ensuing emotional output without forfeiring their turn in the exchange structure.16 The cognitive motivation dictates the syntactic position.

Similarly, the interpersonal marker *you know* serves distinctly different pragmatic functions depending on the emotional register. In low-arousal, conversational states, it may simply seek baseline listener agreement or confirm shared referential knowledge.12 However, under emotional load, *you know* frequently shifts its function to mitigate severe face-threatening acts, display cognitive hesitation, or forcefully supervise turn-shifting to ensure the listener remains engaged in the emotionally fraught topic.13

At the exact moment of peak conviction, a speaker may abandon interpersonal participation markers entirely. The connective tissue is stripped down to aggressive, structural markers (*because, so*) that assert undeniable causal relationships and establish dominant participation frameworks.15 *Because*, for example, shifts from linking two objective events to linking an emotional claim with a subjective justification, supporting the speaker's righteous indignation as a universal truth.15

| ***But*** | Structural | Contrasts two semantic concepts within a single ideational frame. | Rejects the listener's participation framework entirely; forcefully seizes the exchange floor. |

## **Affective Neuroscience: The Arousal-Syntax Relationship**

The observation that an individual's sentence structure fundamentally changes during emotional shifts is not a matter of linguistic style; it is deeply rooted in the neurobiological mechanics of memory, attention, and physiological arousal. Affective neuroscience distinguishes clearly between *valence* (whether an emotion is subjectively positive or negative) and *arousal* (the intensity of the neurophysiological activation, ranging from calming to highly exciting).19 The empirical data from experimental psychology dictates that it is arousal—not merely negative valence—that acts as the primary catalyst for the structural deformation and compression of human language.

### **Arousal-Based Competition and Working Memory Hijacking**

When an individual enters a state of high arousal, such as righteous indignation, the brain's salience network, heavily mediated by the amygdala, is profoundly activated. The amygdala forms dense, reciprocal neuronal loops with the visual cortex, the hippocampus, and the prefrontal cortex, fundamentally altering how information is processed, encoded, and retrieved.21 According to the arousal-based competition model, emotionally arousing stimuli command a disproportionate share of the brain's attentional resources. This hyper-focuses the cognitive apparatus on the central, highly salient details of the emotional event, while simultaneously allowing peripheral details to decay.23

This "attentional narrowing" has severe downstream consequences for the neural systems responsible for language production. The generation of complex grammar—specifically the construction of deep syntactic trees utilizing multiple subordinate and dependent clauses—requires substantial working memory capacity. The brain must hold the main clause in suspension while the subordinate clauses are generated, formatted, and properly attached. Under high arousal, the cognitive resources required to maintain these deep syntactic dependencies are effectively hijacked by the emotional processing centers.23 The brain prioritizes the immediate physiological and emotional threat over the construction of elegant, hierarchical syntax.

### **Syntactic Manifestations: Compression, Subordination, and Imperatives**

The neurobiological constraint on working memory directly and unavoidably results in syntactic compression. The empirical data tracking the arousal-syntax relationship identifies three highly reliable structural modifications that occur under high emotional load 25:

1. **Reduction in Mean Sentence Length (MSL)**: Sentences become measurably shorter. Because the speaker cannot sustain the cognitive scaffolding required for long, meandering explanations, the language becomes clipped, highly localized, and immediate. The attentional narrowing forces the language to deal only with the most salient aspects of the conversational moment.  
2. **Lower Subordination Rates**: The depth of the syntactic tree collapses. Instead of utilizing complex, embedded hypotactic structures (e.g., *Although we previously agreed that the strategy, which was proposed last week, was effective...*), the speaker reverts to paratactic structures. They rely on simple main clauses strung together with basic coordinating conjunctions or zero-copula transitions (*We agreed on the strategy. It was effective. Now it is ruined.*). The sudden disappearance of complex subordination is a primary, reliable biometric indicator of cognitive load induced by arousal.  
3. **Higher Imperative Frequency**: High arousal states inherently prime the human organism for immediate physical or social action (e.g., approach versus avoidance, fight versus flight).26 Linguistically, this manifests as a dramatic spike in imperative verbs (commands). The speaker abandons declarative observations in favor of direct, action-oriented directives, reflecting the neurobiological imperative to resolve the source of the arousal and regain control of the environment.

Furthermore, the lexicon itself undergoes a measurable shift. Studies by Altarriba and Bauer demonstrate that words associated with high emotional arousal possess high "context availability." This means the brain can rapidly and easily generate an imagined situation or circumstance for the emotional word without needing extensive surrounding semantic context.27 Emotion words bypass the slower, standard semantic evaluation pathways and directly trigger rapid affective evaluation, leading to faster cognitive retrieval and deployment in speech.26 Under the load of righteous indignation, the speaker relies on these high-context-availability emotion words to communicate their state efficiently, further reducing the need for lengthy, descriptive sentences.

| **Subordination Rate** | High (Hypotactic structures) | Low (Paratactic structures) | Working memory hijacked by the amygdala; inability to hold complex clauses in suspension. |
| **Imperative Frequency** | Low (Declarative dominance) | High (Command dominance) | Salience network priming the organism for immediate behavioral action or resolution. |
| **Context Availability Dependency** | Low (Can build context via syntax) | High (Relies on highly evocative words) | Bypassing semantic evaluation for rapid affective evaluation. |

## **Computational Extraction: RoBERTa Classifiers and Diagnostic Features**

The transition from theoretical psycholinguistics to automated, scalable extraction relies on advanced computational linguistics. Recent developments (2020–2025) in Transformer-based architectures, spearheaded by researchers such as Verónica Pérez-Rosas, have revolutionized the ability to extract emotional signatures from raw text.29 Unlike early dictionary-based methods (such as the original iterations of LIWC), which counted isolated words without context, modern models utilize contextualized embeddings. Systems like RoBERTa (Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach) leverage deep self-attention mechanisms to understand how a word's psychological and emotional meaning fundamentally changes based on its surrounding syntactic environment.29

### **The Architecture of Continuous Intensity Prediction**

To capture the nuanced, continuous shifts of a coach moving from calm explanation to righteous indignation, simple binary classification (e.g., Angry versus Not Angry) is technologically insufficient. The architecture must predict emotional *intensity* along a continuous regression spectrum. This is achieved computationally by modifying the terminal layers of the RoBERTa model. Instead of a dense layer utilizing a softmax activation function (which outputs discrete probabilities for distinct categories), the intensity model employs a single neuron for each emotion class utilizing a linear activation function.29

The model's optimization is driven by minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function, which calculates the exact deviation between the model's continuous predictions and human-annotated emotional intensity scores across large corpora.29 By fine-tuning these contextual embeddings, the system learns to detect subtle diagnostic features—such as a sudden drop in subordination rate coupled simultaneously with a spike in second-person pronouns—and correctly map this cluster of features to a rising arousal vector indicating indignation.

### **Failure Modes and Ambiguity Resolution**

The accuracy limitations and failure modes of these computational models are highly instructive, as they reveal exactly which linguistic features are robust across populations and which are highly volatile and context-dependent. The computational literature identifies several persistent challenges in emotion classification:

1. **Emotional Overlap**: Models frequently struggle to distinguish between closely related, high-arousal negative emotions, such as basic anger, generalized frustration, and targeted righteous indignation.29 These emotions share overwhelmingly similar linguistic patterns: shorter sentence lengths, high imperative frequency, and outward-facing pronouns. Resolving this overlap requires sequential context modeling, where the system analyzes the preceding and subsequent utterances—often incorporating multimodal cues—rather than evaluating sentences in total isolation.30  
2. **Subtlety and Context Dependence**: Emotions like subtle surprise or quiet disappointment lack explicit lexical markers and rely heavily on pragmatic context, timing, or external world knowledge.29 If a speaker utilizes non-prototypical discourse markers to signal a minor frame shift, a context-free model may completely fail to register the emotional nuance unless it is specifically trained on conversational pragmatics and prompt-aware margin ranking.31  
3. **Co-occurrence and Label Imbalance**: Human emotions are rarely singular. Righteous indignation often co-occurs simultaneously with grief, shock, or betrayal.7 Furthermore, training datasets often suffer from label imbalance, featuring an overrepresentation of basic joy and sadness compared to nuanced states like indignation. This data imbalance requires the implementation of adaptive thresholding techniques. By adjusting post-training margin thresholds, engineers can refine the decision boundaries, preventing the model from lazily defaulting to the majority class when encountering ambiguous connective tissue.29

| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Emotional Overlap (Anger vs. Indignation)** | Shared use of aggressive paratactic syntax and second-person pronouns. | **Sequential Context Modeling**: Evaluating multi-sentence blocks to detect moral framing versus simple goal-blocking. |
| **Intensity Estimation** | Subtle shifts in marker placement without the use of overt emotion words. | **Linear Activation Layers & MSE Loss**: Moving from discrete binary classification to continuous regression modeling. |
| **Context Dependence** | Ambiguous use of markers (e.g., *well* used as a polite hedge vs. an aggressive attack). | **Contextualized Embeddings (RoBERTa)**: Bidirectional self-attention weights that capture the exact syntactic environments of markers. |
| **Label Imbalance** | Rare or complex emotional states are ignored in favor of dominant, simple classes. | **Adaptive Thresholding**: Post-training margin adjustments to intentionally shift decision boundaries for minority emotion classes. |

## **Variables to Extract for the Emotional DNA System**

The deep integration of LIWC function word analysis, Schiffrin’s multi-planar discourse marker pragmatics, Kensinger’s arousal-syntax neuroscience, and Pérez-Rosas’s computational extraction methodologies provides a rigorous, scientifically validated blueprint for the Expression Signature system. The patterns observed in Voice DNA are not arbitrary stylistic preferences; they are the downstream, causal effects of emotional architecture interacting with cognitive load and memory capacity.

To operationalize this science within the 12-step extraction pipeline, the system must continuously monitor a specific census of variables across every spoken interaction. When a coach transitions across emotional registers (e.g., from TTT State A to TTT State B), the system executes real-time extraction across three primary matrices:

### **1\. The Function Word Distribution Matrix**

This matrix continuously tracks the attentional spotlight and cognitive complexity of the speaker, acting as the primary gauge of emotional recovery, stress, and indignation.

* **The Pronoun Ratio (Inward vs. Outward Focus)**: The calculated ratio of first-person singular (*I, me*) to second-person (*you*) and third-person plural (*they*). A rapid, sustained drop in *I* accompanied by a spike in *you/they* triggers an "Indignation/Outward Blame" classification.  
* **The Cohesion Index**: The frequency of first-person plural (*we, us*). Spikes in this variable during high-arousal events indicate a subconscious attempt to forge a shared participation framework and project moral authority onto the listener.  
* **The Analytical CDI**: The density of articles, prepositions, and exclusive words. A declining CDI score indicates an increase in emotional dynamism, a loss of hierarchical processing, and a transition into high cognitive load.

### **2\. The Pragmatic Discourse Marker Census (Pipeline Step 2\)**

This matrix tracks the connective tissue of the speech, focusing not primarily on *which* words are used, but *where* they are placed in the syntactic architecture.

* **Positional Migration**: Tracking the specific movement of cognitive markers (*oh, well*) and structural markers (*because, but*) from internal, mid-clause positions to intonation-unit initial positions. This migration serves as a biometric indicator of frame-shifting and cognitive recalibration under load.  
* **Mitigation vs. Aggression Ratios**: The relative frequency of interpersonal truth markers (*you know, I mean*) utilized to mitigate face-threatening acts, measured against absolute structural markers (*so, because*) utilized to forcefully seize the exchange structure.

### **3\. The Arousal-Syntax Rhythm Signature**

This matrix measures the neurobiological impact of arousal on working memory and language generation, generating the rhythm signature variable.

* **Mean Sentence Length (MSL) Compression**: Real-time tracking of sentence length truncation. A sudden, sustained drop in MSL serves as a primary indicator of amygdala activation and attentional narrowing.  
* **Subordination Depth Collapse**: The measurement of dependent clause embedding. A collapse from complex hypotactic structures (multiple subordinate clauses) to simple paratactic structures (main clauses only) confirms high cognitive load and working memory constraint.  
* **Imperative Action Density**: The frequency of imperative verbs relative to declarative verbs. A spike in imperatives confirms that the arousal has triggered an action-oriented neural state.
