# **Quantitative Convergence of Moral Emotions and Foundations: A Computational Framework for Linguistic Reverse-Engineering**

## **The Algorithmic Alignment Problem in Psychometric Matching**

In the architecture of modern psychometric matching systems and digital coaching platforms, a critical and persistent asymmetry frequently emerges between the measurement of static psychological profiles and the dynamic classification of natural language. In advanced matching engines, the practitioner or coach side is typically evaluated using rigorous, continuously graded psychometric instruments. The premier tool for evaluating ethical and moral cognition in these environments is the Moral Foundations Questionnaire-2 (MFQ-2), which yields a high-precision, quantitative weighting across multiple axes of moral concern. Conversely, the audience or client side, which is evaluated passively through natural language expression and unstructured text input, is often reduced to crude binary classification tags. When an audience member expresses a grievance or a deeply held belief, legacy natural language processing (NLP) systems typically tag the text with a simple boolean identifier (e.g., fairness\_cheating \= TRUE), completely failing to capture the intensity, proportionality, or psychological nuance of the underlying moral cognition.

Bridging this gap requires a structural paradigm shift from discrete boolean classification to continuous algorithmic alignment. To match the precision of the coach-side MFQ-2 output, the audience-side NLP engine must be capable of generating a directly comparable, weighted moral profile across a multi-axis continuum. The computational solution to this challenge lies at the theoretical intersection of three distinct psychological domains: Moral Foundations Theory, the taxonomy of moral emotions, and the computational modeling of emotional cognitive appraisals and their subsequent linguistic signatures.

By understanding that specific moral foundation violations reliably and systematically trigger specific, discrete moral emotions, systems can move beyond broad, generalized sentiment analysis. Furthermore, because these discrete moral emotions follow distinct cognitive appraisal sequences that leave measurable, structural residues in human language, it becomes possible to analyze unstructured text, identify the precise emotional linguistic signature, and mathematically reverse-engineer the exact moral foundation that was violated. This methodology allows the system to determine not only which moral axis has been activated but at what specific intensity. This report details the theoretical mechanisms, linguistic markers, and computational NLP frameworks required to execute this reverse-engineering process, ultimately yielding a weighted moral emotion profile—such as {fairness\_cheating: 0.78, care\_harm: 0.45, loyalty\_betrayal: 0.22}—that perfectly mirrors the quantitative output of the coach's MFQ-2.

## **Theoretical Architecture: The Intuitive Basis of Moral Foundations**

To construct a computational model capable of reverse-engineering moral values from text, it is first necessary to establish the causal vector between moral judgment and emotional affect. Traditional rationalist models of morality, dominated by theorists such as Jean Piaget and Lawrence Kohlberg, posited that moral judgments were the result of deliberate, conscious cognitive reasoning.1 However, the late 20th and early 21st centuries saw a revolution in moral psychology, pivoting away from pure rationalism toward an affect-driven understanding of human ethics.

The Social Intuitionist Model revolutionized this understanding by demonstrating that moral judgments are primarily driven by rapid, automatic, and affective intuitions, with conscious reasoning serving largely as a post-hoc justification mechanism.3 In this model, individuals do not reason their way to a moral conclusion; rather, they feel a sudden flash of affect—a "gut feeling"—and subsequently construct a logical argument to support that feeling.6 This fundamental biological reality dictates that any attempt to measure moral values in natural language must fundamentally focus on the extraction of emotional affect.

### **The Evolutionary Pluralism of Moral Foundations**

Expanding upon the intuitive and affective nature of human morality, Moral Foundations Theory (MFT) posits that human morality is not a monolithic construct but rather a pluralistic framework built upon several innate, evolutionary psychological systems.3 Developed by Jonathan Haidt, Craig Joseph, and Jesse Graham, MFT suggests that these foundations evolved to solve specific social, ecological, and survival dilemmas in ancestral environments, resulting in intuitive ethics that vary in prioritization and weighting across different cultures, political ideologies, and individuals.8

The framework of MFT identifies specific foundational axes that operate as the fundamental cognitive modules of moral judgment. The original and most robustly validated foundations include:

1. **Care/Harm:** This foundation is deeply rooted in the long evolutionary history of mammals, specifically concerning attachment systems and the biological necessity to protect and nurture vulnerable offspring. It underlies the virtues of kindness, gentleness, and nurturance, and is triggered by signs of suffering or distress in others.9  
2. **Fairness/Cheating:** Evolving from the requirements of reciprocal altruism and social cooperation, this foundation drives the human sensitivity to proportionality, justice, and rights. It is activated by situations involving unequal resource distribution, exploitation, or violations of reciprocal social contracts.9  
3. **Loyalty/Betrayal:** This foundation emerged from the history of humans operating as tribal creatures required to form shifting coalitions to compete for resources. It underlies the virtues of patriotism, self-sacrifice for the ingroup, and allegiance, and is triggered by acts of treason or threats to the collective group.9  
4. **Authority/Subversion:** Rooted in primate hierarchical social interactions, this foundation governs deference to leadership, respect for traditions, and the maintenance of social order. It is activated by acts of disobedience, insurgency, or disrespect toward legitimate societal structures.9  
5. **Sanctity/Degradation:** Uniquely evolving from the behavioral immune system and the psychology of pathogen avoidance, this foundation expanded from avoiding physical contamination to encompassing notions of spiritual purity, sacredness, and the elevation of the human body above mere biological functions.9  
6. **Liberty/Oppression:** A later addition to the theory, this foundation relates to the resentment people feel toward those who dominate them and restrict their autonomy, underlying the drive to band together to overthrow bullies and tyrants.3

The critical insight for psychometric matching is that individuals do not rely on these foundations equally. The weighting of these axes forms a unique moral signature for every individual. For instance, progressive ideologies typically rely heavily on the Care and Fairness foundations (often termed the "individualizing" foundations), while conservative ideologies tend to distribute their moral concerns more evenly across all foundations, including Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity (the "binding" foundations).3 The MFQ-2 is specifically designed to capture this weighted distribution quantitatively.12

## **The Taxonomy of Moral Emotions**

While Moral Foundations Theory identifies the cognitive and evolutionary triggers of moral judgment, these triggers do not exist in a vacuum; they are functionally executed through specific affective states known as moral emotions. Moral emotions are distinguished from basic, primary emotions (such as simple fear or surprise) by their inherent link to the interests or welfare of a society, or to individuals other than the agent experiencing the emotion.14 As Haidt notes, moral emotions provide the motivational force—the power and energy—required to sustain human social structures, do good, and punish bad behavior.4

The seminal research by Tangney, Stuewig, and Mashek (2007) categorizes these complex affective states into distinct families based on their valence (positive or negative) and their eliciting conditions (self-focused versus other-focused).14

### **Self-Conscious Emotions**

Self-conscious emotions arise when an individual evaluates their own behavior against internal standards or external societal moral norms.15 This family primarily includes shame, guilt, and embarrassment. Tangney's extensive research highlights a critical functional distinction within this triad that has profound implications for predicting future behavior.

Guilt is characterized by a negative evaluation of a specific behavior (e.g., "I did a bad thing"). Because the focus remains on the specific action rather than the core self, guilt is highly adaptive. It generates tension and remorse, motivating reparative action, apologies, and future adherence to moral standards.14 Guilt-proneness is negatively correlated with recidivism and antisocial behavior.14

Shame, conversely, involves a negative evaluation of the global self (e.g., "I am a bad person"). This totalizing emotional state is overwhelmingly maladaptive. It triggers defensive mechanisms, externalization of blame, social withdrawal, and anger.15 Research has consistently linked shame to a range of problematic behaviors, including higher rates of recidivism, substance abuse, and even physiological manifestations such as T-cell decline in compromised immune systems.14

### **Other-Focused Emotions**

For the purposes of evaluating unstructured audience text—which frequently involves commentary on external events, news, or interpersonal conflicts—the other-focused moral emotions are of paramount importance. These are divided into three sub-families:

1. **Other-Condemning Emotions:** This family includes anger, contempt, and disgust. These are outward-facing, negatively valenced emotions triggered by the observation of moral transgressions committed by others. They serve as the primary enforcement mechanisms for social norms, motivating punishment, ostracization, and the demand for justice.15  
2. **Other-Suffering Emotions:** This family encompasses compassion, empathy, and sympathy. These emotions are elicited by the unmerited suffering or distress of another individual and serve to motivate prosocial, altruistic, and nurturing behaviors.15  
3. **Other-Praising Emotions:** This family includes elevation, awe, and gratitude. These positively valenced emotions are triggered by witnessing acts of uncommon moral beauty, profound self-sacrifice, or receiving unearned benefits. They serve to reinforce positive social bonds and inspire emulation.15

## **The Convergence Matrix: Mapping Foundations to Specific Emotions**

The fundamental mechanism enabling the computational reverse-engineering of moral values is the highly specific, non-random mapping between the violation of a particular moral foundation and the subsequent elicitation of a distinct moral emotion. Individuals do not simply feel a generic state of "anger" or "upset" when encountering a moral transgression. The precise nature of the transgression dictates the specific flavor and topology of the resulting emotional state.

This convergence was initially conceptualized through the CAD Triad Hypothesis (Contempt, Anger, Disgust), which mapped Richard Shweder's three moral ethics (Community, Autonomy, Divinity) directly to three specific other-condemning emotions.19 Modern integrations of MFT and moral emotion research have expanded this triad to encompass all primary foundations, creating a definitive convergence matrix that serves as the theoretical backbone for advanced NLP matching algorithms.

| Moral Foundation Violated | Evolutionary Catalyst | Elicited Moral Emotion(s) | Affective Family | Primary Motivational Action Tendency |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Care / Harm** | Threat to vulnerable kin or innocent individuals | Compassion, Empathic Distress, Pity | Other-Suffering | Nurturance, Protection, Alleviation of suffering |
| **Fairness / Cheating** | Injustice, inequality, free-riding, exploitation | Indignation, Resentment, Righteous Anger | Other-Condemning | Demand for justice, Proportional retaliation, System correction |
| **Loyalty / Betrayal** | Treason, in-group defection, threat to coalition | Outrage, Contempt | Other-Condemning | Ostracization, Group preservation, Severing of ties |
| **Authority / Subversion** | Disrespect, insurgency, hierarchy disruption | Contempt, Cold Anger | Other-Condemning | Enforcement of status, Punishment, Reassertion of dominance |
| **Sanctity / Degradation** | Pathogen presence, spiritual desecration, unnatural acts | Disgust (Physical and Moral), Revulsion | Other-Condemning | Physical distancing, Purification, Total rejection |

This matrix illustrates that the emotional output is inextricably bound to the cognitive input. If an individual encounters a news article describing an unprovoked physical attack on a child, the violation of the Care/Harm foundation reliably triggers intense empathic distress and compassion for the victim.15 If a colleague is promoted unfairly despite contributing less effort, the Fairness/Cheating violation triggers indignation and resentment.15 If an individual desecrates a culturally sacred symbol, the Sanctity/Degradation violation bypasses complex reasoning and immediately triggers physical and moral disgust.9

Consequently, by programmatically identifying the exact sub-type of moral emotion present in a user's text, a computational model can definitively trace the affective state backward to its foundational trigger. The challenge, therefore, lies in how an algorithm can differentiate between closely related emotions—such as indignation and contempt—using only the structure of the text.

## **Cognitive Appraisals: Klaus Scherer's Component Process Model**

To programmatically differentiate between these highly specific moral emotions, it is necessary to deconstruct the subconscious cognitive evaluations that generate them. Emotion is not a monolithic state; it is a dynamic, multicomponent phenomenon. The most robust theoretical framework for analyzing this process is Klaus Scherer's Component Process Model (CPM), which posits that emotional episodes are driven by a continuous, rapid sequence of cognitive evaluations known as Stimulus Evaluation Checks (SECs).5

### **The Sequential Architecture of Stimulus Evaluation Checks**

According to the CPM, an individual continuously appraises a stimulus or event through a rigid sequence of four primary stages. The outcomes of these checks dictate synchronized changes in physiological arousal, motor expression (e.g., facial action units), and subjective feeling.5 The sequentiality of this process is fundamental; appraisals occur in a specific, evolutionary order to ensure cognitive economy, with each check building upon the information gathered in the preceding stage.5

1. **Relevance Detection:** The organism evaluates the novelty, suddenness, familiarity, and predictability of the stimulus. This rapid assessment determines whether the event requires further attention and cognitive processing. If deemed irrelevant, the emotional process terminates.5  
2. **Implication Assessment (Goal/Need Conduciveness):** The individual evaluates whether the event facilitates or obstructs their current goals, fundamental needs, and desires, as well as its intrinsic pleasantness. This stage determines the basic positive or negative valence of the ensuing emotion.5  
3. **Coping Potential Determination:** In this critical stage, the individual assesses their degree of control over the event and their power to alter, adapt to, or manage the consequences. This check determines the trajectory of the response—whether the emotion will drive an active, approach-oriented response (e.g., fight) or a passive, avoidance-oriented response (e.g., flight, submission, or withdrawal).5  
4. **Normative Significance Evaluation:** This is the final, most phylogenetically recent, and most complex check. It represents the definitive divergence point for moral emotions. The individual compares the event and the actions of those involved against internal self-ideals and external social, cultural, and ethical norms.5

The normative significance check is the computational core of moral affect. Basic emotions, such as simple fear or non-moral goal-frustration anger, can be generated without reaching this stage. However, true moral emotions—such as indignation, contempt, and moral disgust—cannot manifest unless an event is appraised as a violation of deeply held societal rules or internal ethical standards.23

### **Differentiating Moral Emotions via SEC Appraisal Profiles**

Because SECs occur on continuous scales, the combinations of appraisal outcomes result in highly specific "modal emotions," each with a distinct appraisal profile.5 Understanding the subtle geometric differences in these profiles allows systems to differentiate between closely related affective states.

| Modal Emotion | Relevance / Suddenness | Goal Conduciveness | Coping Potential (Control/Power) | Normative Significance |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Indignation / Resentment** | High | Highly Obstructive | **High** | High Incompatibility (Injustice) |
| **Contempt** | Low/Medium | Obstructive | **Low** (Target deemed worthless) | High Incompatibility (Hierarchy/Loyalty) |
| **Disgust / Revulsion** | Medium | Irrelevant (Intrinsic Unpleasantness dominates) | **Low** (Avoidance focus) | High Incompatibility (Purity) |
| **Compassion** | High | Obstructive (to another) | Medium/High | High Incompatibility (Unmerited suffering) |

For instance, indignation (associated with Fairness violations) and contempt (associated with Loyalty/Authority violations) are both other-condemning emotions characterized by high relevance, goal obstruction, and high normative incompatibility.5 However, they diverge sharply in the coping potential check. Indignation typically involves an appraisal of high control and high power, driving an active, approach-oriented desire to rectify the injustice, demand restitution, or engage in confrontation.22

Contempt, conversely, features a distinct appraisal architecture. It is characterized by an appraisal of the target's inherent worthlessness or low efficacy. This shifts the coping potential from active confrontation to passive distancing. The desire for reconciliatory action is muted; instead, the emotion drives social exclusion and a permanent restructuring of the relationship hierarchy.29

Disgust and revulsion (associated with Sanctity violations) feature a highly unique appraisal profile. They are characterized by an intense focus on *intrinsic unpleasantness* during the implication stage, which frequently bypasses or overrides complex goal calculations. Coupled with an overwhelming normative incompatibility regarding purity or natural order, the coping strategy is not to change the object or the situation (as in indignation) but to expel, quarantine, or distance oneself entirely from the contaminant.5 Compassion (associated with Care violations) displays an appraisal profile where the goal obstruction is not directed at the self, but is vicariously experienced on behalf of another, paired with a normative appraisal that the suffering is unmerited and unjust.15

## **Linguistic Signatures: The Subconscious Lexicon of Morality**

The cognitive appraisals generated during the rapid SEC sequence do not merely manifest in physiological arousal or facial action units; they leave highly detectable, structural residues in human communication. These residues are not primarily captured in overt vocabulary or semantic declarations (e.g., a person explicitly stating "I am feeling contemptuous") but are structurally woven into the subconscious syntactic and grammatical choices the individual makes while constructing their narrative. The Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) methodology, pioneered by James Pennebaker, provides the architectural framework for extracting these subconscious psychological markers from natural language.32

### **The Psychology of Function Words and Linguistic Distancing**

LIWC operates on the empirically validated consensus that while *content words* (nouns, regular verbs, adjectives) convey the topical semantic meaning of a message, *function words* (pronouns, prepositions, articles, auxiliary verbs, conjunctions) reveal the underlying psychological state, cognitive load, and emotional focus of the speaker.32 Function words account for less than one-tenth of one percent of an individual's vocabulary, yet they constitute over half of the words used in daily speech. Because they carry little semantic weight, they require rapid, automatic cognitive processing to deploy, making them highly resistant to conscious manipulation and highly indicative of true emotional states.36

The concept of *psychological distancing* is paramount in differentiating the moral emotions identified in the CPM appraisal profiles.38 Emotional regulation and the specific coping potential of an emotion directly influence how closely a speaker associates themselves with the subject matter.

When individuals experience intense, approach-oriented emotions driven by high coping potential—such as indignation or resentment regarding a Fairness violation—their language exhibits low psychological distance, or high *linguistic immediacy*. This is marked by an elevated use of first-person singular pronouns ("I", "me", "my"), present-tense verbs, and cognitive mechanism words that indicate an active, ongoing attempt to process the injustice.33 The speaker is cognitively entangled with the transgression and actively trying to resolve it.

Conversely, emotions characterized by low coping potential and avoidance—such as contempt and disgust—manifest through high linguistic distancing. Because the coping appraisal for these emotions dictates social separation, ostracization, and rejection, individuals subconsciously distance themselves from the object of their emotion within their linguistic structure.30 This results in a sharp decrease in first-person singular pronouns, an increased reliance on third-person pronouns ("they", "them", "those"), past-tense verbs, and an uptick in articles ("a", "the") which serve to objectify and depersonalize the subject matter.33

### **Lexical Distributions Across the Moral Affect Spectrum**

Beyond psychological distancing, LIWC captures a multitude of psychological processes—including cognitive load, social orientation, and analytical thinking—that align precisely with the moral convergence matrix. The distinction between a Care violation and a Fairness violation can be mathematically quantified by observing the interplay of these dimensions.32

| Moral Emotion | Foundational Trigger | Key LIWC Dimensions | Structural Linguistic Signature |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Compassion / Empathic Distress** | Care / Harm | High Social Processes, High Negative Emotion (Sadness), High Affiliation | Heavy reliance on first-person plural ("we") and second-person pronouns; elevated use of feeling and perceptual process words; language indicating shared burden. |
| **Indignation / Resentment** | Fairness / Cheating | High Cognitive Processes (Insight, Causation), High Analytic Thinking, High Anger | Elevated cognitive load markers ("because", "should", "ought"); high linguistic immediacy (first-person singular); high absolute/all-or-none language indicating rigid justice boundaries. |
| **Contempt / Outrage** | Loyalty / Authority | High Differentiation, Low Affiliation, High Negative Emotion | High psychological distancing (third-person pronouns); markers of social exclusion and out-grouping; reduction in tentative language reflecting settled hierarchical judgments. |
| **Disgust / Revulsion** | Sanctity / Degradation | High Perceptual Processes, High Negative Emotion (Disgust), Low Cognitive Processes | Extreme psychological distance; increased use of articles (objectification); heavy somatic and body-state terminology; lower analytic complexity indicating instinctual rejection rather than reasoned debate. |

By mapping the output of LIWC dictionaries—specifically analyzing the Categorical-Dynamic Index (a measure of Analytical Thinking), the density of social process terminology, and the ratio of psychological distancing markers—an NLP engine can ascertain the exact nature of the moral emotion being experienced. High analytical thinking combined with high causal terminology ("because", "therefore") and anger words reliably flags a Fairness violation (indignation), as the user attempts to construct a logical argument for why an outcome was unjust.33 Conversely, high sensory/perceptual terminology combined with psychological distancing and negative affect reliably flags a Sanctity violation (disgust), where the cognitive load of rationalization is bypassed in favor of instinctual repulsion.33

## **Advanced NLP Integration: From Binary Dictionaries to Probabilistic Intensity**

While LIWC excels at uncovering the structural and psychological mechanisms of language, modern computational social science requires deeper semantic understanding to accurately capture the specific moral valences of the text. Early computational approaches to Moral Foundations Theory relied heavily on the Moral Foundations Dictionary (MFD). The MFD utilized a rudimentary, binary word-matching algorithm: if a text contained the word "safe," it was tagged with a boolean TRUE for Care; if it contained "fair," it was tagged for Fairness.44

This binary approach proved wholly insufficient for sophisticated matching engines. It lacked contextual awareness, ignored the intensity of the sentiment, suffered from severe vocabulary limitations, and failed to account for the probabilistic nature of language (e.g., failing to distinguish between "it is fair to say" and "this is an unfair system").44

### **The eMFD and MoralStrength Paradigms**

To overcome the limitations of binary classification, computational architectures have transitioned to continuous, multidimensional scoring methodologies utilizing resources like the Extended Moral Foundations Dictionary (eMFD) and MoralStrength.45 These tools were constructed through massive crowdsourcing and expert annotation protocols to assign multidimensional probability distributions to lemmas based on how they are used in actual semantic contexts.

In these advanced computational lexicons, a word is not simply assigned to a single, monolithic foundation. Instead, every word possesses a vector representing its probability of eliciting each of the five moral foundations, accompanied by a continuous valence score (typically ranging from \-1 to \+1) utilizing integration with tools like the VADER sentiment reasoner to indicate the intensity of the virtue or vice expressed.47 When an NLP algorithm processes a document using the eMFD or MoralStrength, it does not count hits; it averages these continuous values across the entire text corpus, yielding a graded moral profile.48 This probabilistic approach correctly models that human moral reasoning is rarely isolated; a single narrative may simultaneously violate the Care foundation at a high intensity and the Fairness foundation at a moderate intensity.47

### **Transformer Models and MoralBERT**

While probabilistic lexicons offer substantial improvements over binary tagging, the highest degree of precision in reverse-engineering moral emotions is achieved through the deployment of transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs). Models such as MoralBERT have been explicitly fine-tuned on heterogeneous datasets to capture the latent semantic relationships, syntactic nuances, and contextual dependencies of moral narratives.45

Unlike dictionary approaches, which fundamentally rely on independent token evaluation, transformer models leverage self-attention mechanisms to understand the relationship between words across long distances within the text. If an individual expresses contempt using sarcasm, complex metaphors, or domain-specific slang—where the constituent words might not inherently belong to an anger or disgust dictionary—the transformer model can still correctly identify the underlying normative appraisal and assign the appropriate emotional vector.44 Furthermore, by projecting the model's neural activations onto predetermined moral axes, researchers can quantify the geometric separability of these foundations, confirming that the models have successfully internalized the multidimensional architecture of human moral cognition and can replicate human-like moral inference.53

## **The Convergence Algorithm: Reverse-Engineering the MFQ-2**

The ultimate objective of this computational framework is to synthesize the theoretical mapping of MFT, the cognitive appraisal mechanics of the CPM, the linguistic signatures of LIWC, and the semantic depth of transformer models into a single, executable algorithmic pipeline. This pipeline must generate an output that is mathematically comparable to the Moral Foundations Questionnaire-2 (MFQ-2).

### **The MFQ-2 Baseline Architecture**

The MFQ-2 is a rigorous psychometric instrument designed to measure an individual's reliance on the foundational intuitions of Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity (often measured via six scales including Equality and Proportionality as subsets of Fairness).12 Rather than relying on simple boolean classifications or flat sums, the MFQ-2 employs a complex Likert-scale design evaluated through Item Response Theory (IRT) and Graded Response Models (GRM).13

This methodology yields a continuous, quantitative psychometric profile. It indicates not just *which* foundations matter to an individual, but the *relative intensity*, *weighting*, and *hierarchy* of each foundation in their moral calculus.13 For a digital coaching engine or matching algorithm to effectively align a coach's MFQ-2 profile with a user's natural language input, the NLP engine must output data in the exact same continuous format: a weighted array of variables spanning the moral axes.

### **The Reverse-Engineering Execution Pipeline**

The process of translating unstructured text into an MFQ-2 equivalent vector follows a strict computational sequence:

1. **Multidimensional Feature Extraction:** The input text is ingested and simultaneously processed through LIWC and a transformer-based encoder (e.g., MoralBERT). The LIWC module extracts the structural ratios of function words, mapping the psychological distance, analytical thinking index (CDI), cognitive load, and baseline affective state of the speaker.32 Concurrently, the encoder maps the contextual semantic representations.  
2. **Appraisal Profiling (CPM Integration):** Using the extracted linguistic markers, the system reverse-engineers the Stimulus Evaluation Checks. If the text exhibits high analytical tone, high cognitive causality words, and first-person linguistic immediacy, the system flags high goal-obstruction coupled with high coping potential.24 If the text displays severe psychological distancing, high objectification, and somatic terminology, the system flags low coping potential and profound normative incompatibility regarding purity.11  
3. **Moral Emotion Classification:** The appraisal profile mathematically categorizes the affective state into a specific moral emotion. The algorithm identifies whether the text represents indignation, revulsion, contempt, or empathic distress based on the coordinates of the SEC outcomes.5  
4. **Intensity Weighting:** The probabilistic lexicons (eMFD/MoralStrength) and MoralBERT outputs are applied to quantify the intensity of the identified emotion. The system calculates a continuous value based on the density, frequency, and semantic gravity of the emotional and moral markers.45  
5. **Foundation Inversion (The Final Output):** Utilizing the theoretical convergence matrix, the classified moral emotion and its calculated intensity are mapped directly back to the foundational violation. High-intensity indignation directly correlates to a heavy weighting in the Fairness/Cheating foundation. Moderate-intensity contempt correlates to a weighting in the Loyalty/Authority foundations.

### **Output Calibration and Precision Matching**

The execution of this pipeline completely bypasses the archaic binary tagging system. Instead of outputting a flat fairness\_cheating \= TRUE tag, the algorithm produces a multi-axis array normalized to mirror the scaling of the MFQ-2. For instance, an input text analyzed by the pipeline might generate the following normalized profile: {fairness\_cheating: 0.78, care\_harm: 0.45, loyalty\_betrayal: 0.22, sanctity\_degradation: 0.05, authority\_subversion: 0.12}.

Because both the audience-side expression engine and the coach-side psychometric engine now operate on identical, graded dimensional spaces, the matching engine can deploy advanced vector mathematics—such as cosine similarity, Euclidean distance, or Mahalanobis distance—to execute matching algorithms with unprecedented precision.55 This ensures that an audience member expressing intense indignation regarding a systemic injustice (a Fairness foundation priority) is not erroneously matched with a coach whose primary moral orientation is bound to hierarchical authority or ingroup loyalty. The result is a fundamental alignment in values, worldview, and cognitive processing, significantly enhancing the efficacy of the coaching interaction.

## **Strategic Conclusions for Algorithmic Alignment**

The transition from binary text classification to continuous, multidimensional assessment represents a critical evolution in computational social science, affective computing, and algorithmic matching. By synthesizing Jonathan Haidt’s Moral Foundations Theory with Tangney’s taxonomy of moral emotions, a clear, causal link is established between the cognitive architecture of human morality and the specific affective states it generates.

Integrating Klaus Scherer’s Component Process Model demonstrates that these affective states are not random phenomena but are the result of highly specific, mathematically distinct cognitive appraisals. Furthermore, leveraging James Pennebaker's LIWC framework confirms that these cognitive appraisals leave measurable, subconscious structural residues in natural language, specifically through the deployment of function words and psychological distancing.

The application of advanced NLP tools, including the probabilistic eMFD and transformer models like MoralBERT, allows systems to detect, quantify, and map these linguistic residues with high fidelity. By utilizing this pipeline to reverse-engineer the text, systems can successfully identify the exact moral emotion being experienced, quantify its intensity, and trace it back to the specific moral foundation that was violated.

The resulting output—a continuous, weighted array of moral cognition—provides total mathematical parity with the rigorous psychometric outputs of instruments like the MFQ-2. This framework eliminates the loss of nuance inherent in binary tagging and ensures that complex algorithmic matching engines can evaluate, predict, and connect human interactions with the full spectrum of psychological depth they require.
