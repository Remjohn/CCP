# **The Architecture of Agency: Engineering Self-Efficacy and Behavioral Persistence through Intelligent Feedback Systems**

The evolution of intelligent feedback systems represents a fundamental shift in the psychological infrastructure of learning and behavior change. As artificial intelligence moves from a passive tool for information retrieval to an active agent of cognitive and behavioral scaffolding, the mechanisms through which it influences human agency have become the subject of intense empirical scrutiny. At the core of this transformation is the integration of three seminal psychological frameworks: Albert Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory, Carol Dweck’s Incremental Theory of Intelligence, and Angela Duckworth’s construct of Grit. This report examines the technical and psychological interplay of AI-generated feedback loops, investigating how consistent, evidence-based interventions enhance perceived capability and support long-term behavioral adherence across education, health, and organizational domains.

## **Theoretical Frameworks of Human Agency and Persistence**

The development of self-efficacy is not a solitary cognitive process but a product of reciprocal determinism, where personal factors, environmental influences, and behaviors interact in a continuous feedback loop.1 Within the context of AI, the environment is increasingly defined by algorithmic agents that simulate the role of mentors, coaches, and peers.

### **Bandura and the Sources of Self-Efficacy in Digital Contexts**

Self-efficacy is the belief in one’s capacity to execute the behaviors necessary to achieve specific outcomes.1 High self-efficacy predicts academic achievement, resilience to stress, and successful health behavior change.1 Bandura identified four primary sources of these beliefs: performance accomplishments (mastery experiences), vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and emotional arousal.1

In AI feedback systems, mastery experiences are facilitated through cognitive load reduction and task decomposition. By processing vast amounts of data and offering decision assistance, AI systems reinforce an individual's perception of their own ability, particularly when the system simplifies mechanical and repetitive tasks.3 This reduction in workload allows employees and students to focus on high-level cognitive functions, thereby increasing their creative self-efficacy and innovative capacity.3 Furthermore, AI usage has been shown to enhance risk-taking behavior in professional settings, with self-efficacy acting as a partial mediator in this relationship.3

Vicarious experiences in digital environments are often represented by peer modeling and exemplar comparisons. AI systems can provide learners with specific examples of work that is within their zone of proximal development, demonstrating that the goal is achievable.4 Verbal persuasion, the third source, manifests as automated encouragement. When delivered by an AI system perceived as credible and empathetic, these "persuasive" messages can significantly raise a user's confidence in overcoming barriers.5 Finally, AI systems can mitigate negative emotional arousal by providing a safe, low-stakes environment for experimentation, reducing the anxiety associated with failure.6

### **Dweck’s Growth Mindset and the "Not Yet" Feedback Loop**

Carol Dweck’s research distinguishes between a fixed mindset—the belief that intelligence is a static trait—and a growth mindset—the belief that abilities can be developed through effort and perseverance.8 AI feedback systems are uniquely positioned to foster growth mindsets by shifting the focus from summative outcomes to formative processes.

Researchers engaging with AI as a "cognitive co-worker" tend to show higher levels of metacognitive awareness.6 By treating the AI as a collaborator rather than a mere tool, users adopt a "not yet" perspective, viewing inaccuracies or early-stage errors as opportunities for iterative refinement.6 This perspective is critical for sustaining motivation in the face of challenges. When AI tools provide immediate feedback on the thought process rather than just the final answer, they reinforce the value of the effort itself, aligning with the core tenets of growth mindset theory.6

### **Duckworth’s Grit and the Role of Deliberate Practice**

Grit, the tendency to sustain interest and effort toward very long-term goals, is a superior predictor of success compared to talent or intelligence.11 The mechanism through which grit leads to achievement is deliberate practice—a highly focused, repetitive, and demanding form of effort that requires continuous, information-rich feedback.13

Digital learning ecosystems, particularly those using AI-driven personalization and immersive simulations, provide the infrastructure necessary for this deliberate practice.15 AI systems can monitor a user's performance with 100% intensity, offering timely refinements that are essential for mastering complex skills.13 By breaking down long-term objectives into manageable steps and regularly adjusting goals based on progress, AI systems help maintain the alignment of values, interests, and time investment that characterizes gritty individuals.12

## **Feedback Typologies and Their Influence on Self-Efficacy**

The impact of a feedback intervention is largely determined by its focus. Empirical evidence suggests a hierarchy of effectiveness among different feedback types, with process-oriented feedback consistently outperforming outcome-oriented approaches in building sustainable self-regulation.10

### **Effort-Based vs. Outcome-Based Feedback**

Outcome-oriented feedback focuses on the accuracy of the final product, identifying the gap between current and desired performance.10 While useful for benchmarking, it often fails to provide the recipient with the strategies needed to close that gap. Process-oriented feedback, conversely, targets the thinking process and strategies used to reach the answer.10

Research in higher education demonstrates that process-oriented feedback is not only more effective for learning but is also perceived more favorably by students.10 Because the process is more under the individual's volitional control than the outcome, feedback focusing on "how to reach the answer" empowers the learner to make meaningful adjustments.10 This sense of control is essential for maintaining intrinsic motivation, especially when effort requirements are high.17

| Feedback Type | Focus | Control Locus | Primary Outcome |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Outcome-Based | Final Result | Low | Identification of performance gaps |
| Process-Based | Thinking Strategies | High | Strategic refinement and skill mastery |
| Effort-Based | Persistence/Exertion | High | Reinforcement of grit and adherence |
| Progress Feedback | Incremental Advancement | Moderate | Sustained engagement in effortful tasks |

### **Progress Feedback and the Effort Discounting Effect**

Progress feedback—information about how much of a task has been completed—serves as a counter-force to "effort discounting." Effort discounting is the phenomenon where the perceived value of a reward decreases as the amount of effort required to obtain it increases.19

Experiments in physical effort-based decision-making show that participants significantly prefer tasks that provide progress feedback.18 Although this feedback may not significantly reduce the subjective rating of the effort itself or improve the efficiency of exertion, its value lies in providing a sense of agency and social motivation.18 In digital environments, progress updates (such as probability bars or real-time completion percentages) help users manage their cognitive resources and maintain focus throughout long-term endeavors.18

## **Frequency, Consistency, and the Cyclical Model of Feedback**

The timing and regularity of feedback are critical variables in the motivation equation. Automated systems offer a level of frequency and consistency that is unattainable by human mentors.

### **The Cyclical Behavioral Model of Self-Feedback**

Recent scholarship conceptualizes feedback not as a single intervention but as an ongoing, cyclical process of refinement.4 In this model, the student or professional intentionally engages in a loop of monitoring, inquiring, comparing work with exemplars, and implementing revisions.4

| Stage of Cycle | Cognitive Process | AI Role |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Monitoring** | Engagement with external comments | Data processing and presentation |
| **Inquiry** | Requests for clarification (Feedback Inquiry) | Natural language interaction (Chatbots) |
| **Evaluative Comparison** | Assessment against standards | Intelligent tutoring/Exemplar retrieval |
| **Planning/Revision** | Formulating improvement strategies | Personalized action planning |

4

High-frequency feedback systems are strongly associated with higher levels of self-belief and teacher/student confidence.21 Regular evaluations and consistent constructive comments help individuals recognize the value of their progress and adapt their strategies effectively.21 In digital contexts, this "micro-coaching"—brief, in-the-moment situations where feedback is provided during the task execution—is a prime target for automated systems.22

### **Feedback Self-Efficacy (FSE) as a Mediator**

A pivotal finding in recent structural equation modeling is the role of Feedback Self-Efficacy (FSE). FSE is the belief in one's competence to meaningfully interpret, evaluate, and implement feedback.4 Research indicates that while process feedback (PF) contributes indirectly to academic proficiency, its effect is mediated through FSE.4

Students with high FSE are more likely to proactively seek, process, and use feedback across different subject domains.4 This suggests that the primary goal of an AI feedback system should be to enhance the user's belief that they *can* use the provided information to improve. Interestingly, gender differences have been noted in these pathways: for male students, process feedback has a stronger predictive power over FSE, while for female students, the link from FSE to actual academic achievement is more pronounced.4

## **Personalization: The Engine of Adherence**

AI-driven personalization represents a structural shift from static rule-based systems to adaptive models that continuously learn from user data. By providing specific recommendations and unique content based on individual preferences, AI enhances engagement and reduces friction in the behavior change process.23

### **Dynamic Profiling and Real-Time Adaptation**

Modern AI systems collect a wide range of behavioral signals, including website activity, interaction timing, device type, and engagement history.25 This data allows the system to shift from "periodic updates" to "continuous adaptation".25 For example, in health interventions, dynamic personalization can adjust the timing of reminders or the tone of motivational messages based on the user's real-time context and past responsiveness.24

Personalized experiences directly impact behavioral outcomes. Organizations using AI personalization see significant increases in conversion rates (15-25%) and customer retention (15%).24 In the context of self-efficacy, these personalized interactions make users feel "valued and understood," which leads to higher satisfaction and a stronger commitment to long-term goals.24

### **Hyper-Personalization and Behavioral Intention**

Hyper-personalization leverages generative AI to create customized headlines, visuals, and calls to action that resonate with specific target audiences.25 In health behavior change, this manifests as AI chatbots providing "multimodal reasoning" and "conversational flexibility".5 Studies show that AI chatbots based on motivational interviewing (MI) are perceived as more empathetic and trustworthy than traditional directed interventions.5 These MI-based systems significantly raise participants' self-efficacy to overcome barriers and positively impact their intrinsic motivation for physical activity and other healthy behaviors.5

## **Comparative Analysis: Automated vs. Human-Delivered Feedback**

The debate over the "human element" in coaching and education has been reframed by the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). While human coaches possess advantages in expressing empathy and building deep personal connections, automated systems provide unique strengths in persistence and autonomy support.27

### **Empathy, Consistency, and Persistence**

In randomized trials comparing automated health coaching to text-based human practitioners, both groups reported positive experiences.27 Human coaches were more effective at using probing questions to tailor support and addressing the "holistic" context of a user's life.27 However, human coaches also encountered significant barriers in digital mediums, such as difficulties gauging user investment and struggles with the persistence required for continuous monitoring.27

The chatbot coach, conversely, demonstrated advantages in being consistently persistent and providing users with multiple options, which fostered a sense of client autonomy.27 Autonomy is a critical component of Self-Determination Theory, and its support is essential for long-term self-management.27 Furthermore, recent evaluations of ChatGPT-generated weight loss coaching messages found that, after initial prompt refinement, AI messages matched human-written ones in perceived helpfulness.31 In Phase 2 of these studies, 50% of the AI-generated messages were misidentified by users as being human-written, suggesting that the "empathy gap" is narrowing.31

### **The Hybrid and Multi-Agent Paradigm**

The emerging consensus points toward a symbiotic relationship between human and automated approaches. "Humbot" or hybrid systems utilize AI for routine monitoring and "micro-coaching"—providing brief support in the moment—while falling back to human operators for complex or high-stakes requests.22 This approach harnesses the scalability and accessibility of digital health interventions (DHIs) while maintaining the meaningful engagement of human coaching.22

| Feature | Human-Led Feedback | AI-Automated Feedback | Hybrid Approach |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Scalability** | Low | High | High (AI-fronted) |
| **Consistency** | Variable | Absolute | High |
| **Empathy** | High/Authentic | Moderate/Simulated | High (Human oversight) |
| **Autonomy Support** | Moderate | High (Option-focused) | High |
| **Long-term Sustainability** | Low (Resource-heavy) | High (Cost-effective) | Moderate/High |

## **Domain-Specific Evidence: Education and Professional Development**

The effectiveness of AI in building self-efficacy is particularly well-documented in academic and corporate learning environments.

### **Meta-Analytic Trends in AI and Self-Efficacy**

A comprehensive meta-analysis of studies conducted between 2005 and 2025 indicates that AI usage has a significant positive impact on learners' self-efficacy, with an overall effect size (Hedges’ g) of 0.758.32 This impact is moderated by several contextual factors:

1. **Discipline:** The strongest effects were observed in Natural Sciences and Medical disciplines (Q \= 10.348), where tasks are often structured and outcomes are verifiable.32  
2. **Role of AI:** AI systems functioning as "intelligent learning tools" for self-study produced higher self-efficacy gains than those acting in "mixed" roles or purely as tutors.32  
3. **Learner Level:** University students experienced significantly higher benefits (g \= 0.813) compared to K-12 students, likely due to higher levels of existing metacognitive maturity.32

### **Growth Mindset in Research and Higher Education**

In postgraduate environments (MPhil/PhD), researchers who embrace AI as a "cognitive partner" or "thinking partner" demonstrate enhanced resilience and self-evaluation.6 AI tools serve as a "practice ground" for iterative feedback, allowing researchers to refine their argumentation and structure before seeking supervisor feedback.6 This "scaffolding for independence" boosts autonomy and confidence, viewing the research process as a progressive act of building rather than a one-off performance.6

Furthermore, AI-mediated language learning platforms have shown significant success in improving students' speaking self-efficacy and grit in the context of English as a Foreign Language (EFL).33 In these environments, grit acts as a fundamental psychological driver of communicative action, supporting sustained effort in the face of inevitable challenges.33

## **Long-Term Behavioral Adherence and Health Interventions**

In the domain of health behavior change, AI systems are increasingly used to manage sedentary behavior, weight loss, and chronic illness management.

### **AI-Based Personalized Exercise Recommendation Systems (AIPER)**

Sedentary behavior is a major public health concern associated with chronic diseases and poor cognitive performance.35 Longitudinal studies (6-month periods) on AIPER systems demonstrate their ability to dynamically sense individual differences in real time and generate tailored exercise prescriptions.35 When the interaction is intuitive and feedback is timely and clear, users develop increased confidence and a greater willingness to persist in health-related actions.35 This extended Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) highlights that affective attitudes and motivational mechanisms are critical for sustained system engagement.35

### **The Risks of Over-Reliance and Delayed Care**

While trust in AI enhances engagement, excessive reliance can lead to detrimental health behaviors.36 "AI trust" has been associated with increased odds of delayed care, particularly among patients with chronic conditions who may prioritize AI-generated symptom evaluations over traditional clinical advice.36 Therefore, the design of AI health tools should prioritize "calibrated decision support" rather than full automation, balancing the convenience of digital advice with the necessity of human clinical intuition.36

## **Design Principles for Agentic AI Systems**

To systematically build self-efficacy and support persistence, AI feedback systems must be designed with human agency as the primary objective.

### **Preserving "Struggle" and Skill Development**

As AI becomes more capable of executing tasks instantly, there is a risk of "cognitive offloading" and "metacognitive laziness," where users retain no knowledge once the AI is removed.38 A key design principle is to "preserve struggle when delegation is effortless".38 This involves helping users identify which mental work is worth keeping because the process itself has value for skill development and personal expression.38

Future AI systems should be designed as "transparent, process-visible thinking partners" rather than "faster answer engines".38 Exposing intermediate reasoning and uncertainty helps reduce "over-trust" and supports responsible use.20 Furthermore, "metacognition should be the interface," where the system helps users decide what to think about versus what to delegate.38

### **Feedback and Control Mechanisms**

Effective AI design includes structured, explicit feedback mechanisms that allow users to rate outputs on specific dimensions like accuracy and tone.39 This guided flow helps users articulate issues clearly and provides better signals for the AI's iterative improvement.39 Additionally, providing "confidence scores" (e.g., percentage probabilities) helps users gauge the reliability of an output and decide when verification is necessary.20

| Design Principle | Function | Impact on Self-Efficacy |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Transparent Reasoning** | Shows the "why" behind the answer | Enhances strategic understanding |
| **Confidence Scores** | Calibrates user trust | Supports critical evaluation/Metacognition |
| **Input Guidance** | Prompts for better user inputs | Reduces error-induced frustration |
| **Progressive Disclosure** | Reveals complexity in steps | Prevents cognitive overload |
| **Source Attribution** | Links to evidence/citations | Builds credibility and trust |

## **Computational Foundations: Reward Functions and Reinforcement Learning**

The "behavior" of an AI system is governed by its reward function—a numerical score that tells the agent which actions lead to "good" results.40

### **Aligning AI Goals with Human Persistence**

In reinforcement learning (RL), an agent learns by doing, maximizing the cumulative reward received from its environment.40 To support self-efficacy, reward functions must be carefully formulated to represent task-critical performance indicators without encouraging "reward hacking" (where the agent finds a shortcut to a high reward without completing the actual task).40 For example, in autonomous systems, reward functions incorporate safety, progress, and comfort to guide optimal behavior.41

More recently, research has introduced "self-judging" LLMs that can effectively self-improve without reference solutions or human guidance.42 By leveraging the asymmetry between generating and verifying solutions, these models provide their own reward signals, identifying weaknesses and generating targeted practice.42 This autonomous cycle of self-evaluation and iteration is the computational analog to the growth mindset.

### **Modeling Self-Efficacy Updates**

Self-efficacy can be modeled as a dynamic learning rule within an RL agent. For a Q-learning agent, self-efficacy is often calculated as the integral of reward prediction errors (RPEs), which modulates the agent's expectation of future outcomes.43

## **The Bidirectional Dynamics of Human-AI Interaction**

Human-AI interaction is increasingly viewed as an iterative cycle where both parties mutually exchange information and adapt their mental models.44

### **Shared Understanding and Joint Action**

The perception of "shared understanding" (PSU) is crucial for effective collaboration.45 PSU occurs when the information in one agent's mental model matches the knowledge in another's.45 AI systems can simulate "joint action" by mimicking user phrasing and adapting to context, creating the illusion of mutual responsiveness even without shared mental states.46

This linguistic reciprocity and psychological proximity calibrate trust and allow interactants to anticipate each other's needs.45 However, these interactions can also reshape the human brain. The same social brain systems governed by trust, mentalizing, and bonding are activated during AI interactions.47 Prolonged interaction with personalized AI companions can alter social brain circuits and behavioral judgements, highlighting both the therapeutic promise for reducing loneliness and the peril of unintended neuroplastic changes.47

### **Bias Amplification and the Feedback Loop**

A significant risk in human-AI feedback loops is the "snowball effect" of bias amplification.48 AI systems can exhibit biased judgements, which are then internalized by the human user. This internalization leads to further errors in judgment, which are then fed back into the AI system as data, creating a loop where small initial errors escalate.48 Participants are often unaware of the extent of the AI's influence, making them highly susceptible to this reinforcement of perceptual and social biases.47

## **Conclusion: Engineering for Sustainable Change**

The integration of AI feedback systems into the landscape of human behavior change offers an unprecedented opportunity to systematically build self-efficacy and support long-term adherence. By grounding these systems in the theoretical work of Bandura, Dweck, and Duckworth, designers can move beyond simple information delivery to create agentic scaffolds for human potential.

The most effective systems are characterized by:

1. **Process-Oriented Feedback:** Focusing on thinking strategies and effort rather than just outcomes, which enhances perceived control and strategic mastery.  
2. **High-Frequency Cyclical Interaction:** Providing continuous "micro-coaching" that builds Feedback Self-Efficacy as a central mediator of success.  
3. **Hyper-Personalization:** Utilizing dynamic behavioral signals to tailor support, thereby increasing satisfaction and reducing adherence friction.  
4. **Autonomy-Supportive Automation:** Leveraging the persistence and consistency of AI while maintaining human oversight for holistic and empathetic support.  
5. **Agency-Preserving Design:** Ensuring that the AI acts as a thinking partner that "preserves struggle" and supports metacognitive resource allocation.

As AI continues to become more deeply embedded in daily life, the challenge remains to maximize its positive impact on human resilience while minimizing the risks of over-reliance and bias amplification. The future of self-efficacy development lies not in replacing human effort with algorithmic ease, but in using the power of intelligent feedback to make "the hard way" a visible, achievable, and dignified choice for personal growth.
