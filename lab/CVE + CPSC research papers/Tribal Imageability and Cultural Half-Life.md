# **Tribal Imagen Activation: Measuring the Psychological Potency and Cultural Half-Life of Concrete Nouns in Subculture-Specific Visual Communication**

## **Theoretical Genesis: The Evolution of Dual Coding and the Concreteness Advantage**

The contemporary understanding of lexical processing and mental representation is fundamentally anchored in the Dual Coding Theory (DCT) proposed by Allan Paivio in the early 1970s and substantially refined in his seminal work, *Mental Representations* (1986). Paivio posits that the human cognitive architecture is uniquely specialized to deal simultaneously with language and non-verbal objects or events through two functionally and structurally independent, yet interconnected, symbolic systems. The verbal system handles linguistic input and output via units known as "logogens," which are organized in associative and hierarchical networks. Conversely, the non-verbal system processes mental images and perceptual information through units termed "imagens," which are organized in terms of part-whole relationships and retain the continuous, analog features of the original stimuli.

The structural independence of these systems implies that each can operate without the direct involvement of the other; however, the referential connections between logogens and imagens allow for a word to evoke an image and an image to evoke a verbal label. This referential processing is the cornerstone of the "concreteness effect," a robust experimental finding demonstrating that concrete nouns—those referring to tangible, sensory-perceptual entities like "bicycle" or "apple"—are recalled and recognized with significantly higher accuracy and speed than abstract nouns such as "justice" or "belief". The empirical explanation for this phenomenon is that concrete words are dual-coded, activating both the verbal and imagery systems simultaneously, thereby providing two independent retrieval routes in memory. In contrast, abstract words rely almost entirely on single-coding within the verbal system.

Historical psycholinguistic research has consistently confirmed that the dual-activation advantage is strongest for nouns with high imageability scores, which represents the ease and speed with which a word arouses a sensory experience or mental image. While concreteness and imageability are highly correlated, often showing coefficients near 0.83, they are not conceptually identical. Concreteness is defined by the degree to which a word refers to a physical object, while imageability is a measure of the psychological ease of image generation. Research indicates that instructions for imageability norming often produce a predominantly visual bias, where participants focus on mental pictures at the expense of other sensory modalities like sound or touch.

| Psycholinguistic Unit | Representational Format | Organizational Principle | Functional Role |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Logogen** | Sequential, Symbolic | Hierarchies and Associations | Verbal processing and output |
| **Imagen** | Holistic, Analog | Part-Whole Relationships | Imagery and non-verbal events |
| **Referential Connection** | Cross-modal | Inter-system Mapping | Image-to-word translation |
| **Associative Connection** | Intra-modal | Intra-system Network | Word-to-word or image-to-image links |

A critical limitation in existing imageability research is the treatment of imageability as a stable, universal property of language. Standard psycholinguistic databases, such as the Medical Research Council (MRC) Psycholinguistic Database, derive their norms from general populations, assuming that the mental "imagen" evoked by a word is consistent across all speakers of a language. This research proposal challenges this assumption by introducing the construct of Tribal Imageability. Tribal Imageability suggests that the degree to which a concrete noun activates a specific, charged imagen is conditioned by the shared lived experience, values, and specialized lexicons of defined subcultures. In coaching subcultures, for instance, nouns may carry a "tribal charge" that elevates their psychological potency far beyond their generic dictionary definitions.

## **Taxonomy of Specialized Coaching Tribes and Tribal Lexical Charging**

The coaching industry is not a monolith but a collection of distinct "tribes," each defined by a unique semantic field and a set of high-charge tribal nouns. This research focuses on four specific coaching segments: conscious business coaches, high-performance executive coaches, healing and transformation coaches, and financial freedom coaches. Each tribe functions as a moral and linguistic community where specialized vocabulary maintains group boundaries and performs social identity.

The "conscious business" subculture is grounded in an organizational philosophy that enterprises exist to create sustainable value for people and the planet, rather than solely for profit. Leaders in this tribe are encouraged to act from "authentic values" and practice "unconditional responsibility". Nouns such as "resonance," "congruence," "integrity," and "wholeness" are not abstract concepts for these practitioners; they are concrete, charged symbols of an aspirational professional state. For this tribe, the word "impact" activates a specific imagen of societal transformation, whereas for a general business audience, it may merely signify revenue growth.

In the high-performance executive coaching tribe, the focus shifts to individual leadership capacity, emotional intelligence, and the maximization of results within large organizations. The lexicon here is defined by nouns such as "leverage," "optimization," "scaling," and "bottleneck". The imagen of a "bottleneck" in this context is a high-charge diagnostic framework for decision paralysis or delegation failure. While "leverage" is a common business term, for the high-performance tribe, it refers specifically to the "multiplier effect" of a capable leader operating across an entire organization.

The healing and transformation coaching segment utilizes a lexicon rooted in somatic intelligence and trauma-informed recovery. Practitioners in this space speak of "relational alchemy," "ruptures," "vagal toning," and "breakthroughs". These nouns serve as "conceptual pegs" that anchor the individual's journey from a state of repression to one of embodied empowerment. For members of this tribe, the noun "trauma" is not an abstract medical term but a concrete imagen of nervous system dysregulation that impacts leadership and communication.

Finally, the financial freedom coaching subculture, including the FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) movement, revolves around a lexicon of economic autonomy. High-charge nouns in this tribe include "asset," "dividend," "cash flow," and "wealth-building". Here, "freedom" is not a philosophical ideal but a quantifiable state reached through specific savings rates and debt elimination strategies. The word "asset" activates a charged imagen of a self-sustaining income generator, a meaning that is deeply embedded in the lived experience of the FIRE community.

The psychological potency of these nouns is mediated by their ability to trigger identity-level recognition. When a subculture member encounters a high-TIRS noun, the activated imagen corresponds to an essentialist "That is exactly who I am" response. This is distinct from "behavioral recognition," where a person identifies a familiar action ("I do that") without a deep identity connection. Identity-level responses are associated with higher emotional arousal, stronger brand loyalty, and increased willingness to pay, making them the primary goal of visual content hooks in the Conscious Visual Engine.

## **Phase 1: Developing the Tribal Imageability Rating Scale (TIRS)**

The first phase of the research focuses on the "concrete noun extraction problem": identifying which nouns within a body of coaching text possess high tribal imageability and which have become culturally abstracted despite their grammatical concreteness. To address this, the study will develop and validate the Tribal Imageability Rating Scale (TIRS), adapting the methodology of the MRC Psycholinguistic Database. The MRC database, originally published by Max Coltheart (1981), is a computerized resource providing information on 26 different linguistic properties for over 150,000 words. Imageability and concreteness ratings in the MRC are typically derived from merging several sets of existing norms, such as those from Paivio et al. (1968), Toglia and Battig (1978), and Gilhooly and Logie (1980).

The standard MRC norming procedure involves asking naive human subjects to judge the imageability of isolated words on a 7-point Likert scale. TIRS modifies these instructions for subculture-specific application. Participants will be asked to rate a corpus of 600 nouns not just on general imageability, but on three tribal dimensions: how specific, charged, and exclusively associated with their subculture's lived experience each noun is.

1. **Tribal Specificity**: The degree to which the noun's meaning is tied to the specialized domain of the coaching tribe.  
2. **Affective Charge**: The emotional intensity and identity resonance of the activated imagen.  
3. **Dictionary Exclusivity**: The distance between the noun's tribal meaning and its generic dictionary definition.

The study will recruit 320 participants, consisting of 80 individuals from each of the four identified subcultures (conscious business, high-performance, healing, and financial freedom). This balanced design ensures that the resulting TIRS norms are statistically robust and representative of the distinct linguistic realities of each segment. The 600-noun corpus will include high-potency candidates identified through social media text analysis, alongside generic concrete nouns from the MRC database to serve as control stimuli.

| TIRS Dimension | Instruction Focus | Rating 1 (Low) | Rating 7 (High) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Specificity** | Subculture Association | Used across all industries | Unique to this coaching tribe |
| **Charge** | Emotional Intensity | No identity connection | "This is exactly who I am" |
| **Exclusivity** | Meaning Divergence | Standard literal meaning | Coded tribal shorthand |

The resulting TIRS database will constitute the first empirically validated set of tribal imageability norms. Validation will involve comparing TIRS scores to standard MRC imageability and concreteness ratings to identify nouns where tribal potency significantly exceeds universal norms. For instance, the word "shadow" may have moderate imageability in the general population (referring to light obstruction), but for the healing and transformation tribe, it is expected to receive a near-maximum TIRS score due to its high charge and specificity regarding "shadow work" and trauma resolution. This granular data is essential for the data infrastructure of the Conscious Visual Engine, which requires text elements with currently active tribal potency to guarantee the performance of visual recipes.

Analysis of the Phase 1 data will also look for bimodal distributions within tribal ratings. In universal imageability studies, a bimodal distribution suggests that participants are employing different decision criteria, such as focusing on visual features versus emotional arousal. Within a subculture, a bimodal distribution may indicate a "lexical innovation imperative" where the tribe is in the process of replacing an older, bleached term with a newer, more potent alternative.

## **Phase 2: Longitudinal Tracking of the Cultural Half-Life**

The second phase of the research addresses the "half-life problem": the rate at which tribal imageability degrades over time as cultural exposure shifts a noun's associative network. This phenomenon, known in sociolinguistics as semantic bleaching, occurs when a word becomes less specific and powerful through overuse and its application in contexts that do not provide it with its originally intended power. Corporate appropriation—the process by which corporations identify subcultural slang as marketable and adopt it in generic advertising—is a primary catalyst for semantic bleaching. When a term like "authenticity" is used broadly in car advertisements or fast-food slogans, it loses its "subcultural capital" and its value as an identity marker of inclusion for the original innovators.

To quantify this decay as a continuous process, Phase 2 utilizes a longitudinal design tracking 150 high-TIRS nouns identified in Phase 1 across a 24-month observation period. The primary data source is a corpus of 50,000 coaching content posts per month, gathered from major social media platforms. The core of this analysis is Shannon entropy, a measure derived from information theory that describes the amount of "choice" or uncertainty associated with a symbol in a string.

In natural language processing (NLP), Shannon entropy can be used to measure "contextual diversity"—the diversity of semantic environments in which a word appears. Contextual diversity has been shown to be a more effective predictor of lexical processing speed and accuracy than simple word frequency; the lexicon is theorized to be organized according to "need," where words occurring in more diverse contexts are more accessible in memory.

## **Phase 3: Cognitive Recognition Modes and Identity Hook Potency**

Phase 3 tests the practical hypothesis that high-TIRS concrete nouns in visual content hooks produce measurably different recognition response patterns compared to low-TIRS or generic concrete nouns. This phase introduces a critical empirical distinction between identity-level recognition and behavioral recognition. Behavioral recognition, often termed "I do that" recognition, is characterized by the identification of familiar actions or routines without deep self-relevance. Identity-level recognition, or "That is exactly who I am" recognition, involves the activation of a salient social identity, heightening the consumer's self-importance and creating a profound sense of self-congruence with the brand or message.

To distinguish between these two recognition modes, the study employs eye-tracking combined with retrospective verbal protocol (RVP). Eye-tracking turns visual attention into a measurable behavioral signal, providing precise data on the number of fixations and fixation duration. Fixation duration is interpreted as an index of the degree of cognitive effort and the complexity of information processing; longer durations on an Area of Interest (AOI) indicate greater interest or deeper semantic elaboration. Identity-level recognition is hypothesized to produce longer and more intense fixations on tribal nouns within visual content hooks.

Retrospective verbal protocol—where informants state their thoughts after completing the task while watching a playback of their own eye movements—allows researchers to uncover content-specific thoughts that are inaccessible to eye-tracking alone. Unlike concurrent think-aloud (CTA), which has been empirically proven to significantly distort eye-tracking data by increasing cognitive load and disrupting task flow, RVP provides independent evidence of behavior and reasoning.

| Measure | "I do that" (Behavioral) | "That is who I am" (Identity) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Primary AOI Gaze** | Functional features of the visual | Tribal noun/Charged imagen |
| **Fixation Duration** | Short, scanning pattern | Long, focused, intense |
| **Verbal Report Code** | Habit, routine, objective | Values, self-expression, subjective |
| **Neural Trigger** | Standard recognition patterns | "Ignition-like" Gamma power strike |
| **Response Valence** | Neutral to slightly positive | High affective valence (Happy face advantage) |

The neurophysiological basis for these recognition modes is further explored using insights from ECoG (electrocorticography) and backward masking experiments. Reaching perceptual awareness of a recognizable visual object is associated with an abrupt, "ignition-like" increase in broadband gamma power (30–70 Hz) and evoked responses in high-order visual areas. The proposal suggests that high-TIRS tribal nouns function as "identity cues" that trigger this ignition more readily for tribe members than for the general population.

Participants will view static visual content "recipes" from the Conscious Visual Engine, each containing either a high-TIRS tribal noun, a low-TIRS specialized noun, or a generic concrete noun. Retrospective reports will be coded using linguistic markers of identity. Identity-level responses will be characterized by:

* **Essentialist Statements**: "This reflects my soul," "This is me".  
* **Affective Arousal**: Descriptions of feelings rather than actions.  
* **Aspirational Goal-Linking**: Connecting the visual to a deep sense of shared purpose or "higher calling".

In contrast, behavioral responses will focus on:

* **Functional Intent**: "I should buy this tool," "I need to do this task".  
* **Routine Identification**: "I have seen this before in my work".  
* **Strategy Analysis**: "This is a good way to scale".

This empirical distinction will provide the first robust baseline for measuring the "identity-hook" effectiveness of visual communication, ensuring that text elements in visual recipes produce the deep resonance required for effective coaching transformation.

## **Implementation of the Tribal Imagen Activation Registry**

The final output of the research is the Tribal Imagen Activation Registry, a dynamic data infrastructure designed to integrate with the Conscious Visual Engine. This registry will house the validated TIRS norms and the tracked decay curves for the 150 core tribal nouns. The Registry provides the formal protocols for lexical refreshes, guaranteeing that text elements in visual recipes draw from nouns with currently active tribal potency rather than culturally expired or generic associations.

Modern computational models of imageability often dismissal fundamental properties of subculture-specific language, relying instead on bag-of-words methods or generic image-caption datasets. The Tribal Imagen Activation Registry solves this by providing "vertical domain knowledge" through entropy-optimized dynamic text segmentation (EDTS). EDTS aims to maintain semantic integrity while improving the efficiency of visual-linguistic retrieval.

The implementation pipeline follows a seven-stage process:

1. **Ingestion**: Systematic crawling of monthly coaching content (50,000 posts).  
2. **Segmentation**: Applying EDTS algorithms to form chunks where tribal nouns maintain their high-potency context.  
3. **Potency Filtering**: Cross-referencing extracted nouns against the validated TIRS norms.  
4. **Decay Monitoring**: Calculating Shannon entropy (![][image1]) to detect signals of bleaching or genericization.  
5. **Steered Sampling**: Injecting a continuous semantic-similarity penalty to discourage the use of redundant or bleached outputs.  
6. **Refresh Triggering**: Automatically flagging tribal nouns for replacement once they reach a predefined entropy threshold.  
7. **Engine Interface**: Providing current high-charge "lexical hooks" to the Conscious Visual Engine for content generation.

## **Diachronic Evolution and the Lexical Innovation Imperative**

The longitudinal findings from Phase 2 will provide deep insights into the "lexical innovation imperative"—the necessity for subcultures to continuously develop new terms to maintain group boundaries as their existing lexicon is appropriated by mainstream corporate actors. This process is not random but follows a predictable pattern of "renewal," where construction is weakened or lost (bleaching), and a new construction is "pulled" into the system to revive its force.

Shannon entropy analysis of the coaching corpus will reveal how nouns such as "sustainability" or "mindfulness" have moved from high-charge tribal signals to generic placeholders. As these terms bleach, they are often used with broader "potential productivity," occurring with a wider variety of non-specialized elements. For example, "mindful" may have narrowed from its meditative roots to become a generic synonym for "considerate" or "careful" in corporate HR discourse. In response, the subculture must innovate terms with higher "indexical mileage"—semiotic markers that are harder to de-racialise or de-contextualise.

The research also identifies the "Uncanny Valley" of corporate language use, where a brand's attempt to adopt subcultural jargon results in an exaggerated or misfake performance that creates disapproval and cynicism in the target audience. By tracking the Shannon entropy of high-TIRS nouns, the Registry can signal when a word has crossed into this "Uncanny Valley," allowing the Conscious Visual Engine to avoid terms that are no longer authentic to the tribe.

This "continuity of change" is a fundamental law of linguistic evolution. Language adapts to reduce "fuzziness" and satisfy momentary communicative needs, but the strategic devaluation of meaningful terms by market forces accelerates this decay. The Registry's refresh protocols are a direct response to this "political economy of language," providing a scientific tool for the preservation of subcultural wisdom and the maintenance of a shared epistemic foundation within the coaching segments.
