# **Modeling Narrative Identity as a Dynamic Arc in Serialized First-Person Spoken Accounts: A Computational Framework**

The conceptualization of personal identity within the behavioral and psychological sciences has undergone a profound paradigm shift, moving away from rigid, static categorizations toward highly dynamic, temporally bound frameworks. Traditionally, identity has been measured through the lens of dispositional traits, which represent stable attributes and behavioral tendencies that remain relatively consistent across varying contexts and the lifespan.1 However, this trait-based approach is theoretically ill-equipped to capture the highly malleable, adaptive nature of human cognition during periods of active intervention, such as therapy, targeted coaching, or structured behavioral rehabilitation.1 To fully comprehend how individuals change, contemporary psychology relies on the Life Story Model of Identity, pioneered by Dan P. McAdams. This model defines narrative identity as an internalized, continuously evolving story of the self that an individual crafts to integrate their reconstructed past, perceived present, and imagined future, thereby providing their life with a necessary degree of unity, meaning, and purpose.4

In the context of high-frequency, serialized self-reflection—such as daily voice journaling over a 30-day period—individuals do not merely report objective events; they actively engage in autobiographical reasoning and narrative reconstruction.2 When computational systems treat identity as a static label derived from an isolated "I am" statement, they capture only a momentary snapshot of the self-schema, missing the underlying psychological scaffolding that drives actual behavioral transformation.10 The academic literature provides robust empirical evidence that the structural and thematic components of these self-narratives can be systematically extracted, that these components fluctuate over surprisingly short temporal windows, and that specific narrative trajectories serve as leading predictive indicators of real-world behavioral change.11 Moving beyond basic semantic or sentiment analysis toward the computational extraction of validated narrative dimensions allows for the precise, longitudinal mapping of a subject's psychological evolution.

## **Validated Structural Components of Personal Narrative Identity**

To successfully model narrative identity as an evolving arc rather than a categorical label, the specific, extractable dimensions that constitute the life story must be rigorously operationalized. The academic literature, spearheaded by McAdams, McLean, Pals, Pasupathi, and others, has standardized several distinct thematic and structural categories that serve as the fundamental building blocks of narrative identity analysis.13 These validated dimensions are broadly categorized into overarching motivational themes, sequential affective trajectories, and metrics of integrative autobiographical reasoning.

### **Motivational Themes: Agency and Communion**

Drawing upon foundational psychological theories regarding the duality of human existence, narrative psychology evaluates the life story through the primary lenses of Agency and Communion.4 These superordinate thematic clusterings articulate the narrator's core psychological needs, goals, strivings, and interpersonal orientations.4 In manual coding frameworks, these themes are evaluated based on their presence and intensity within specific autobiographical episodes, such as high points, low points, and turning points.17

The theme of Agency refers to the degree to which the protagonist is autonomous, exerts control over their environment, and possesses the power to affect their own life trajectory.6 In highly agentic narratives, the individual is depicted as the active author of their circumstances, demonstrating self-determination rather than acting as a passive victim of external forces or fate.20 The validated coding manual for Agency delineates this construct into four distinct, extractable subthemes.17 The first subtheme is Self-Mastery, which involves the protagonist striving to successfully master, control, and perfect the self, demonstrating profound internal regulation and insight.17 The second subtheme is Status or Victory, where the narrative emphasizes winning, achieving heightened social status, or emerging victorious in a competitive or challenging environment.1 The third subtheme, Achievement and Responsibility, is detected when the individual overcomes significant obstacles to achieve instrumental goals or assumes a high degree of responsibility in a given social or professional context.1 Finally, the Empowerment subtheme is coded when the protagonist is enhanced, strengthened, or empowered by an external force, a recognized authority, or a profound internal realization.17

Conversely, the theme of Communion captures the narrator's motivation to form intimate connections, participate in a larger social organism, and experience a sense of togetherness and harmony with others.4 The validated subthemes for Communion similarly break down into four distinct categories.17 The primary subtheme is Love and Friendship, characterized by the explicit expression of deep affection, intimacy, or the formation of close platonic or romantic bonds.17 The Dialogue subtheme focuses on reciprocal, meaningful communication and the sharing of perspectives between the protagonist and another individual.17 The Care and Help subtheme is identified when the protagonist provides physical, emotional, or psychological nurturance, assistance, or support to others, often reflecting generative concerns.17 Lastly, the Unity and Togetherness subtheme reflects a profound sense of belonging to a larger community, family unit, or social ecosystem, highlighting the integration of the self into a collective identity.17

### **Affective Trajectories: Redemption and Contamination Sequences**

Beyond basic motivational drives, narrative identity is heavily defined by the trajectory of affective sequences—specifically, how the narrator links the emotional valence of sequential events across time. The two master narratives of navigating adversity in American culture are the Redemption sequence and the Contamination sequence.22

A Redemption sequence occurs when a demonstrably negative, adverse, or painful event is narratively transformed into a positive outcome or a state of personal enhancement.6 In these sequences, the narrator finds a "positive seed" in the midst of suffering, ensuring that the final affective tone of the episode is redemptive rather than despairing.23 The standardized coding scheme for redemption sequences further subdivides this phenomenon into specific categories of growth.25 These include Enhanced Agency, where the negative event leads to greater self-efficacy; Enhanced Communion, where suffering brings the individual closer to others; and Ultimate Concern, where the adversity yields profound existential or spiritual clarity.25

In stark contrast, a Contamination sequence dictates a trajectory where an initially positive, joyful, or acceptable state is subsequently spoiled, ruined, or overwhelmed by a negative event.6 Within this cognitive framework, the negativity is perceived as an inescapable pollutant that permanently erases the preceding positivity, often resulting in a fatalistic worldview.23 While redemptive narratives are consistently linked to generative behavior, psychological maturity, and sustained behavioral change, contamination sequences are strongly and positively correlated with trait neuroticism, clinical depression, and general psychological stagnation.23

### **Themes of Integrative Meaning and Autobiographical Reasoning**

The final critical dimensions of narrative identity pertain to autobiographical reasoning, which is the sophisticated cognitive process of drawing semantic connections between past life events and the current state of the self. These dimensions evaluate the structural and interpretive depth of the narrative rather than merely cataloging its thematic content.8

Meaning-Making evaluates the extent to which the narrator extracts value, learning, or philosophical growth from an experience.21 The academic coding manuals explicitly differentiate between two levels of this construct: "lesson learning" and "gaining insight".2 Lesson learning represents a shallower, highly pragmatic form of meaning-making that results in specific behavioral adaptations for similar future situations, without fundamentally altering the individual's self-concept.21 Gaining insight, however, requires profound cognitive restructuring, where the narrator infers a deeper, generalized truth about their core identity, their relationships, or the nature of the world at large.2

Complementing this is Exploratory Narrative Processing, which measures the richness, depth of thought, and complexity of the narrator's engagement with their own memories.6 High exploratory processing indicates that the individual is engaged in active self-exploration, considers multiple perspectives or conflicting emotions simultaneously, and remains open to positive self-transformation.15 Conversely, low exploratory processing is characterized by defensive minimization, rigid conformity to existing beliefs, or narrative foreclosure, where the individual ceases to seek new meanings from their experiences.31

## **Computational Detection of Narrative Themes in Transcribed Spoken Language**

Historically, the extraction of McAdams' narrative dimensions required human coders trained to rigorous standards of inter-rater reliability, a process that is incredibly resource-intensive and difficult to scale.13 However, recent advancements in Natural Language Processing (NLP), stylometry, and the deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) have rendered the automated, computational extraction of these complex psychological constructs highly viable.34 This is particularly relevant when processing the inherently unstructured, disfluent data typical of transcribed spoken language from voice journals.

### **The Transition from Lexical Methods to Large Language Models**

Early computational narratology relied heavily on rigid keyword-based classifiers, dictionary-based tools such as Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC), and foundational machine learning algorithms including Support Vector Machines and Naïve Bayes.22 For instance, initial attempts to automate the detection of redemption and contamination sequences utilized product score models and chi-square selection to extract discriminating keywords associated with a "tendency of change".22 While these lexical methods were effective at detecting surface-level emotionality—such as tracking the ratio of first-person singular pronouns ("I") to first-person plural pronouns ("We") to estimate shifts between individual agency and group communion—they ultimately struggled to capture the semantic depth, contextual nuance, and complex temporal arcs inherent in autobiographical reasoning.38

The introduction of transformer-based LLMs—such as OpenAI's GPT-4, Anthropic's Claude 3 and 4 families, and fine-tuned models like distilBERT—has fundamentally altered the landscape of automated narrative coding.33 These models possess the extensive context windows and sophisticated inferential reasoning capabilities necessary to apply highly abstract psychological coding manuals directly to raw, unstructured text.35 In a pivotal 2024 empirical study assessing the viability of LLMs for narrative-personality assessment, researchers provided models like GPT-4 and Claude-2 with expert-refined prompt inputs based directly on complex psychological rating manuals, such as the Social Cognition and Object Relations scales.33 The results conclusively demonstrated that when supplied with highly detailed, theoretically grounded instructions, these LLMs achieved inter-rater reliability and absolute agreement scores that rivaled gold-standard human clinical raters.33

Furthermore, automated methods leveraging LLMs can evaluate texts along multiple continuous dimensions simultaneously. They are capable of parsing out subtle levels of exploratory processing, causal coherence, and agentic framing without the cognitive fatigue that traditionally limits human annotators.41 By utilizing prompt engineering strategies that operationalize theoretical constructs into structured Information Extraction (IE) templates, systems can achieve high F1-scores in identifying multiple experiential expressions and thematic configurations across varied narratives.44

### **Addressing the Nuances of Transcribed Voice Journals**

Extracting narrative identity from serialized voice journals presents unique computational challenges and opportunities compared to traditional written essays. Spoken language is characterized by spontaneous disfluencies, non-linear thought progression, conversational syntax, and a distinct stylometric footprint.37

However, transcribed speech captures critical micro-linguistic markers of cognitive processing that are frequently edited out of written text. Stylometric analysis of spoken Self-Defining Memories (SDMs) has proven highly effective in distinguishing specific psychological profiles and tracking emotional regulation.37 For instance, the spontaneous use of tense shifting during a spoken narrative—specifically, an abrupt shift from the past tense to the present tense when describing a difficult or traumatic event—serves as a highly reliable computational marker of trauma reliving and spatial-temporal immediacy.46 This shift from narrative-based memory to image-based memory is heavily linked to the narrator's current capacity for meaning-making and their overall psychological well-being.46

To effectively operationalize McAdams' framework on transcribed voice data, a computational pipeline must combine advanced Semantic Role Labeling (SRL) with LLM-based zero-shot or few-shot classification.40 SRL identifies the deep thematic roles within a spoken sentence, isolating the "actor" from the "acted upon".40 By computationally analyzing syntactic agency bias—such as whether the speaker utilizes active forms ("I decided to leave the situation") versus passive or distancing forms ("The situation forced me out")—the system can quantitatively track the micro-genetic emergence of the "Self-Mastery" subtheme well before it coalesces into a macro-narrative shift.20 Additionally, tracking linguistic metrics such as lexical diversity, lexical density, and syntactic cohesion in transcribed speech provides an automated proxy for measuring the structural complexity and exploratory processing dimensions of narrative identity.37

## **The Temporal Dynamics of Narrative Identity: Evidence for Short-Term Shifts**

A persistent, foundational misconception in certain branches of personality psychology is that narrative identity, much like the Big Five dispositional traits, remains highly stable over time and only evolves gradually across the lifespan.28 However, narrative identity occupies the psychological level of "characteristic adaptations"—the dynamic cognitive and behavioral strategies an individual uses to interpret their environment and meet the everyday demands of life.1 Because narrative identity is continuously reconstructed in real-time as new events occur, it is highly sensitive to contextual disruption, meaning-making exercises, and targeted behavioral interventions.48

### **Detectable Shifts within 21 to 30 Days**

The academic literature emphatically confirms that an observation period of 21 to 30 days is entirely sufficient to detect statistically significant shifts in narrative identity, provided the individual is engaged in active, structured reflection or a dedicated intervention program.32

Longitudinal studies tracking daily diary entries and daily measures of identity demonstrate that narrative commitments and self-concept clarity fluctuate visibly on a weekly basis, rather than remaining static over years.49 In studies focusing specifically on 21-day and 30-day expressive journaling protocols, researchers have noted that this exact duration functions as an optimal temporal window for habituation and initial cognitive restructuring.51 During the first week of daily journaling, personal narratives typically exhibit low structural coherence, high negative emotionality, and a lack of clear meaning-making.51 However, by the third and fourth weeks of continuous practice, participants demonstrate a measurable, computational transition from passive emotional venting to active meaning-making, producing texts that feature significantly higher rates of narrative agency, self-reflection, and psychological integration.51

Furthermore, evidence from short-term cognitive-behavioral coaching and narrative therapy interventions confirms this rapid timeline. Significant increases in narrative agency—specifically the transition from viewing oneself as a passive victim of circumstances to an active protagonist capable of initiating change—have been documented to emerge rapidly, often between the fourth and eighth sessions of structured intervention.11 When individuals participate in high-frequency daily voice journaling, the data density (e.g., 30 distinct assessment points in a single month) far exceeds the temporal resolution of traditional weekly therapy. This high-resolution data allows computational models to capture the micro-genetic emergence of insight and agency in near real-time, mapping the exact days where narrative foreclosure gives way to exploratory processing.49

### **The Psychotherapy Trajectory and Lagged Growth Curve Evidence**

The definitive empirical evidence for the rapid malleability of narrative identity, and its precise timing relative to behavioral change, derives from Jonathan M. Adler’s multiwave longitudinal investigations into short-term personality change. Adler (2012) tracked adult participants over a 12-session course of psychotherapy, collecting rich personal narratives at 12 distinct assessment points (after every session) while concurrently tracking mental health metrics.2

The findings from analyzing these nearly 600 narratives unequivocally demonstrated that the theme of agency increased dramatically over the course of the short-term treatment, while the theme of narrative coherence remained relatively stable.11 Crucially, Adler's research proved that the narrative architecture does not change *after* the individual gets better; rather, the narrative architecture changes *first*. Using sophisticated lagged growth curve models, Adler demonstrated that increases in narrative agency occurred chronologically prior to the associated improvements in clinical mental health.2 This finding remained highly robust across all participant demographics and baseline personality traits, establishing that narrative identity reconstruction operates on a rapid, session-by-session timeline during active psychological work.2 Similar temporal dependency has been observed in treatments for Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD), where a reduction in problem emotion-based narrative markers between session 1 and session 5 strongly predicted symptom reduction assessed between session 5 and session 10\.60 This body of research proves that a 30-day window is not only sufficient to detect changes, but is actually the critical period where the foundational narrative shifts that precipitate behavioral change take root.

## **Predictive Validity: Narrative Themes and Real-World Behavioral Change**

The transition from assigning a static identity label to tracking an evolving narrative arc is theoretically and practically valuable primarily because narrative dimensions possess extraordinary predictive validity. Specific thematic configurations do not merely reflect an individual's current emotional state; they act as leading, predictive indicators of future behavioral change, goal attainment, and psychological recovery in real-world contexts.61

### **Agency as the Primary Precursor to Behavioral Activation**

As established by longitudinal lagged modeling, increasing linguistic markers of agency constitute the most reliable narrative predictor of impending psychological and behavioral improvement.2 In coaching and therapeutic environments, when a client begins to spontaneously shift their syntax and thematic focus from passive victimhood to active ownership—demonstrating an uptick in the "Self-Mastery" and "Achievement/Responsibility" subthemes—the practitioner (or computational system) can reliably predict subsequent symptom reduction and goal realization.20

Within the framework of Intentional Change Theory (ICT), a primary model for executive coaching, success relies entirely on moving an individual from a negative emotional attractor (the undesired current self) to a positive emotional attractor (the desired ideal self).63 Goal attainment in this context is viewed not merely as the execution of a discrete task, but as a direct byproduct of identity expansion and the reconstruction of the narrative self.5 When computational models detect a sustained, multi-day upward trajectory in agentic phrasing across a 30-day voice journaling protocol, the system is actively identifying the precise cognitive scaffolding required for the user to execute and sustain real-world behavioral modifications.5 Without this preceding shift in narrative agency, behavioral changes are highly likely to be transient.

### **Redemption Sequences and Sustained Recovery**

The presence of redemption sequences within the narrative arc is highly predictive of sustained, long-term behavioral change, particularly in contexts requiring immense self-regulation and habit disruption, such as substance use disorder (SUD) recovery, trauma rehabilitation, and major life transitions.32 Studies tracking adults in addiction recovery programs reveal that individuals whose narratives feature a strong redemption sequence—where the trauma or failure of addiction is deliberately recontextualized as a necessary catalyst for profound personal growth—are statistically far more likely to maintain long-term sobriety and positive health outcomes than those lacking this specific thematic structure.2

Conversely, the detection of "narrative foreclosure" or frequent contamination sequences serves as a critical, predictive warning sign. Narrative foreclosure is the expressed psychological belief that the protagonist's story is effectively over, that they cannot change, and that the future is immutably closed.32 In longitudinal observations of SUD treatments, future-directed narrative foreclosure is highly associated with an increased probability of post-treatment relapse.32 If a computational analysis of a serialized voice journal detects a sudden plateau in exploratory processing combined with a rise in contamination sequences—where minor daily setbacks are narrated as ruining overall progress—it signals an impending behavioral regression.32 Identifying this pattern computationally allows a system to intervene before the behavioral relapse physically occurs.

### **Accommodative Processing vs. Assimilative Processing**

The subthemes of meaning-making also hold distinct predictive power depending on their cognitive depth. When a narrator engages solely in "assimilative" meaning-making—extracting a basic, pragmatic lesson without altering their overarching worldview or self-schema—the predictive outcome is generally limited to short-term hedonic well-being, effectively acting as a temporary mood boost.2

However, when automated analysis detects "accommodative" exploratory processing—characterized by deep self-reflection, high narrative complexity, and the gaining of profound "insight"—the data strongly predicts long-term eudaimonic growth and ego development.2 Accommodative processing indicates that the user is actively integrating difficult realities and complex emotions into a more mature, resilient identity structure. This depth of narrative processing results in highly stable behavioral changes that are capable of withstanding future environmental stressors and identity threats.2

## **Conclusion and System Architecture Implications**

The academic consensus surrounding Narrative Identity Theory provides a rigorous, empirically validated framework for transitioning automated coaching and wellness systems away from assigning static identity categories toward tracking dynamic, evolving narrative arcs.

By leveraging advanced Large Language Models combined with specific, prompt-engineered instructions derived directly from Dan McAdams' validated coding manuals, computational systems can accurately extract the core dimensions of narrative identity from transcribed spoken language. The tracking of these specific variables—Agency, Communion, Redemption sequences, Contamination sequences, and Exploratory Processing—over a 30-day serialized voice journaling protocol is highly scientifically sound. The literature confirms that 21 to 30 days of consistent, reflective output provides sufficient data density to observe significant, statistically measurable shifts in characteristic adaptations and narrative framing.

Most importantly, modeling identity as a continuous narrative trajectory unlocks unparalleled predictive capabilities. Because measurable shifts in narrative agency and the emergence of redemptive meaning-making consistently occur *prior* to real-world behavioral improvements, analyzing the micro-genetic evolution of the life story day-by-day allows a system to anticipate breakthroughs, predict behavioral regressions, and dynamically adapt its interventions to match the user's precise stage of cognitive reconstruction. Implementing this sophisticated framework transforms a serialized journaling platform from a passive repository of daily events into a highly sensitive, predictive instrument of psychological and behavioral change.
