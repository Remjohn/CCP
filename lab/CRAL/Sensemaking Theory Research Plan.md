# **The Architecture of Enacted Meaning: A Comprehensive Analysis of Sensemaking Theory and its Application to Research Frameworks**

The fundamental paradigm shift in organizational cognition, initiated by Karl E. Weick, posits that organizations and the individuals within them do not inhabit a pre-existing, objective environment to which they merely react. Instead, they are the primary architects of the very environments they seek to understand. Sensemaking, as a theoretical construct, describes the complex cognitive and social processes through which people structure the unknown so as to be able to act within it.1 It is the ongoing, retrospective development of plausible images that rationalize what people are doing, shifting the analytical focus from how decisions shape organizations to how meaning drives the process of organizing itself.2 This report provides an exhaustive examination of sensemaking theory, tracing its evolutionary roots from early social psychology to modern operational updates, and validating its core claims for application in high-stakes research environments.

## **The Ontological Foundations of Organizing**

The genesis of sensemaking theory lies in the transition from viewing an organization as a static, architectural entity to conceptualizing "organizing" as a continuous, dynamic activity. In the seminal work *The Social Psychology of Organizing* (1979), the focus shifted toward the "double interact" as the basic unit of social behavior.4 This perspective suggests that social order is not a byproduct of pre-ordained goals but an emergent property of interlocking behaviors and cognitive maps.5

Organizing is essentially a process of managing ambiguity, uncertainty, and equivocality.5 The evolutionary model of sociocultural change—variation, selection, and retention—was mapped onto human activity to explain how organizations survive.5 In this framework, "variation" is replaced by "enactment," where people "put something out there" into the environment. "Selection" involves the construction of meaning around those actions, and "retention" is the preservation of those meanings as organizational memory or dogma.5 The profound implication of this model is that organizations are often closed systems, responding primarily to the consequences of their own previous enactments rather than external stimuli.5

The evolutionary process within organizations operates through the "sensemaking recipe": "How can I know what I think until I see what I say?".5 This simple phrase captures the retrospective core of human cognition. Action precedes thought; people act, and then look back at the results of those actions to determine what they were intending to do.4 Consequently, the primary function of an organization is to provide "pretexts for storytelling" and "keep people busy," creating a shared narrative that allows collective action to continue despite an inherently unknowable world.5

## **The Seven Properties of Sensemaking**

The internal mechanism of sensemaking is defined by seven intertwined properties that establish how meaning is constructed. These properties do not function as a linear sequence but as a self-contained set of research questions that define the boundaries of human cognition within organizations.9

### **Identity Construction and the Self-Concept**

Sensemaking is fundamentally grounded in the construction and maintenance of identity. The individual doing the sensemaking is not a neutral observer; they are a participant whose self-concept determines what they notice and how they interpret events.11 People have multiple, shifting identities that they project onto their context to see what "version" of themselves is most plausible in a given situation.2 In organizational crises, the inability to adapt one's identity often leads to a collapse of meaning. For example, in the Mann Gulch disaster, firefighters refused to drop their tools because their identity was so deeply tied to being a "firefighter with tools" that to drop them felt like a loss of self, rendering the act of survival through flight nonsensical.13

### **The Retrospective Lens of Meaning**

Retrospection is the property that distinguishes sensemaking from traditional models of prospective planning. Human experience is a continuous flow of "streaming" events; meaning can only be attributed to this flow after the fact, when a moment is "chopped out" and examined.7 The retrospective nature of sensemaking implies that "people can know what they are doing only after they have done it".4 This creates a temporal gap where action leads and interpretation follows, ensuring that our understanding of the present is always a reconstruction of the past.7

### **Enactment as Environmental Creation**

Enactment is the process where individuals actively construct the reality they face. Rather than passively perceiving an environment, actors "enact" it through their actions, which then produce the very constraints and opportunities they encounter.7 This is particularly evident in strategic choices: an organization that enacts a "hostile" environment by engaging in aggressive price wars will eventually face a hostile reality as competitors respond in kind.12 The environment is thus an output of organizational activity, not a pre-existing input.8

### **Social Context and Collective Mind**

Sensemaking is never a solitary activity. Even when an individual is thinking alone, their thoughts are shaped by the social norms, language, and expectations of the groups to which they belong.9 Organizations function as "collective minds" formed through shared experiences and intersubjectively shared meanings.12 The social context binds people to actions they must then justify, creating a framework of "heedful interrelating" where the actions of one member are understood in relation to the whole.8

### **The Ongoing Nature of Experience**

Sensemaking never starts or stops; it is a continuous process of acquisition, reflection, and action.12 Because experience is a constant flow, individuals are always in the middle of a story, revising and updating their interpretations as new situations arise.12 Stability in an organization is not a natural state but a continuous "accomplishment" that requires constant effort to maintain against the incessant flux of the environment.12

### **Extraction of Cues**

Because the world is an "overflow" of information, the mind must extract specific "cues" to focus on. These cues are simple, familiar structures that serve as seeds from which a larger sense of what is occurring is developed.11 The frames we inherit—ideologies, organizational premises, or tradition—determine which cues we notice and which we ignore.9 Sensemaking is the process of connecting these extracted cues to larger frames to form a coherent account.9

### **Plausibility over Accuracy**

Perhaps the most critical property for actionable research is the prioritization of plausibility over accuracy. In complex, ambiguous situations, complete accuracy is impossible and often paralyzing.8 People search for an answer that is "good enough" to enable action—a process similar to "satisficing".13 A plausible story provides a map that allows for organizational movement to continue, even if the map is not perfectly accurate. If the story is believable and enables action, it is more valuable than a "correct" but inactionable analysis.2

## **Enactment: Action vs. Discovery in Organizational Practice**

In the Weickian framework, the distinction between "action" and "discovery" is the pivot point of organizational success. Conventional theory suggests that researchers and managers "discover" meaning in a pre-existing environment. Sensemaking theory counters this by establishing that "people discover their own inventions".8 This is the essence of enactment: the actor creates the conditions under which the answer becomes visible.7

### **The Roomy Framework of Enactment**

Enactment is described as a "roomy framework" that reminds organizational members that they are not victims of their environment but producers of it.12 By taking a specific action, an organization "brackets" a portion of the continuous stream of experience and turns it into a "situation" that can be comprehended in words.16 This transformation of "circumstances" into a "situation" is the primary site where meaning materializes.3

The environment is not separate from the organization; it is "partly created by it".12 Strategic choices do not just react to a competitive landscape; they shape it. Innovation does not just fill a market gap; it enacts a new market that then requires further sensemaking to navigate.12 In this cycle, action is not the final step of a decision process but the first step of a cognitive one.6

### **The Dissolution of the Theory-Practice Dichotomy**

When organizations are viewed as an ongoing activity of sensemaking, the dichotomy between theory and practice dissolves.6 The organization "lives" in its environment, and its ability to interpret that environment is directly tied to its ability to act within it. Interpretations determine effectiveness, and the purpose of organizational design is to facilitate these interpretations rather than just to facilitate decision-making.6 This shift highlights that "people act and then they interpret," reversing the classical notion that people decide and then act.6

## **Belief-Driven vs. Action-Driven Sensemaking**

The process of constructing meaning can be initiated either by beliefs or by actions. These two drivers represent the dual engines of sensemaking, often operating in a recursive loop to stabilize the organization's world-view.

### **Belief-Driven Sensemaking: Arguing and Expecting**

When sensemaking is driven by beliefs, it typically takes the form of arguing or expecting.21

* **Arguing**: This occurs when there is a disparity of beliefs in a social context. Members engage with one another to test the validity of their internal frames and justify their subjective views.9 The discourse generated during an argument is itself an act of sensemaking, as it forces the articulation of cues and the negotiation of a shared narrative.  
* **Expecting**: This is the continuous effort to relate current experiences to pre-existing expectations. Beliefs project onto the future, forming expectations that guide what people notice.9 Cues that are inconsistent with expectations are often ignored or reframed to preserve the integrity of the belief-system, until the "threshold of dissatisfaction" is reached and a "shock" triggers a shift in frame.18

### **Action-Driven Sensemaking: Committing and Manipulating**

Action-driven sensemaking focuses on the retrospective justification of behavior.

* **Committing**: When an individual takes a voluntary, public, and irrevocable action, they become bound to that action. To maintain a coherent identity, they must retrospectively construct a plausible justification for why they acted in that way.21 Commitment thus "binds people to actions that they then must justify," and these justifications constrain future explanations.8  
* **Manipulating**: This is the proactive form of enactment. Sensemakers "manipulate" the environment to see what kind of reactions it produces.12 These reactions then become new cues for sensemaking. This continuous cycle of actor-environment engagement is self-fulfilling: actions are taken to justify beliefs, and beliefs are updated to justify actions.21

| Driver Type | Primary Mechanism | Organizational Example |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Belief-Driven (Arguing)** | Engagement to justify internal frames.21 | Boardroom debates over a new market entry strategy.9 |
| **Belief-Driven (Expecting)** | Relating experiences to future projections.21 | A team ignoring initial signs of product failure due to optimism.18 |
| **Action-Driven (Committing)** | Justifying public, irrevocable behaviors.21 | An executive doubling down on a failed merger they championed.22 |
| **Action-Driven (Manipulating)** | Staging environment to see responses.21 | A startup launching a "beta" to see how users enact the tool.12 |

## **Operationalizing Sensemaking: The 2005 Update and Beyond**

The 2005 refinement by Weick, Sutcliffe, and Obstfeld sought to make sensemaking more future-oriented and behaviorally defined. It defines sensemaking as the site where "meanings materialize that inform and constrain identity and action".3 This update emphasizes that sensemaking is not just about understanding the past; it is a "springboard into action".2

### **From Decision-Making to Sensemaking**

A pivotal contribution of this era is the shift away from a traditional focus on decision-making toward the processes that constitute the meaning of those decisions.2 In uncertain and ambiguous situations, the critical question is not "What should we decide?" but "What is the story?".13 Once the story is constructed and a plausible explanation is reached, the subsequent decision often becomes obvious. Sensemaking provides the "clear questions and clear answers" that feed the decision-making process.23

### **The Process of Organizing under Uncertainty**

In high-reliability organizations, sensemaking is used to "impose order on chaos" during non-normal events.13 The process involves:

1. **Noticing and Bracketing**: Selecting specific information from the environment.12  
2. **Labeling and Categorizing**: Using language to stabilize the experience.12  
3. **Action Continuation**: Generating a story that allows the organization to keep moving.13

Small instances of sensemaking can have massive results if they are retained and replicated throughout the organization.13 Conversely, high-power individuals have an outsized influence on this process, as their "labels" for events often become the official organizational narrative.13

## **Information Seeking: Bridging Theory to Practice**

Brenda Dervin’s work (1998) serves as the bridge between organizational sensemaking and the practice of information seeking. Dervin reconceptualizes knowledge from a "noun" (an object to be retrieved) to a "verb" (an active process of construction).24

### **The Situation-Gap-Use Methodology**

Dervin’s model is built on a fundamental metaphor for human behavior in time and space:

* **Situation**: The context of the user where their current understanding is functioning.  
* **Gap**: The "rupture" or "shock" where the person can no longer move forward because their current sense has failed.24  
* **Use (or Help)**: The meaning that is constructed to bridge the gap and enable the person to continue their journey.26

This model places the user, rather than the information system, at the focus of the research.26 It acknowledges that reality is "gappy" and that humans live in a state of perpetual "sense-making and sense-unmaking".24 Knowledge management, in this view, is about prioritizing "people over technology" and managing the "cacophony of diversity" without homogenizing it.25

### **Collaborative Sensemaking in Information Seeking**

Information seeking is often an interactive and infinite process.26 In workplace settings, users do not follow a sequential order; their needs are ambiguous and their focus shifts as they extract new cues from the information they find.26 Sensemaking in this context is a "quest for coherence," where the user is constantly testing the "plausibility" of the information relative to their specific situation.28

## **Alternative Perspectives: The Data-Frame Model**

The work of Klein, Moon, and Hoffman (2006) provides a critical, operationally testable perspective on sensemaking, particularly for intelligent systems. They define sensemaking as a "motivated, continuous effort to understand connections" between people, places, and events to anticipate their trajectories.29

### **Functionality of the Data-Frame Model**

The Data-Frame Model (DFM) suggests that sensemaking is a cycle of:

* **Data Exploration**: Looking for cues in the environment.  
* **Frame Construction**: Creating mental models to explain the data.28

Unlike Situation Awareness (SA), which is about the *state* of knowledge achieved, sensemaking is about the *process* of deliberating over alternative plausible explanations.29 It is driven by a "drive to comprehend" and is often a social activity that promotes common ground.29 The DFM is particularly useful for extracting "testable elements" of expertise, as it identifies how experts explore data to improve the plausibility of their explanations while explaining anomalies.28

| Concept | Klein’s Sensemaking | Endsley’s Situation Awareness |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Nature** | A process of achieving outcomes.31 | A state of knowledge achieved.31 |
| **Focus** | Strategies, connections, and barriers.31 | Data elements, inferences, and predictions.31 |
| **Function** | Testing/improving plausibility.29 | Maintaining current state knowledge.31 |
| **Unit** | Deliberating over alternative explanations.29 | Achievement of memory representation.31 |

## **The Collapse of Sensemaking: Resilience and Failure**

One of the most profound insights of sensemaking theory is the analysis of its collapse. A "cosmology episode" occurs when the universe is no longer perceived as a rational, orderly system.13 When sensemaking fails, organizations unravel, often with catastrophic results.

### **The Anatomy of a Cosmology Episode**

The Mann Gulch disaster serves as the primary case study for the collapse of sensemaking. The collapse occurred in several stages:

1. **Surprise and Shock**: The sudden appearance of the fire challenged the team's understanding of reality.13  
2. **Nonsensical Events**: The foreman’s actions (starting an "escape fire" and ordering the team to drop tools) contradicted the team’s scripts and organizational identity.13  
3. **Role Collapse**: The system of roles failed, leading to a state of panic because members could no longer identify their place in the collective mind.13  
4. **Failure of Wisdom**: The team relied on "definite closure" rather than a healthy understanding that their knowledge was incomplete, leading them to underestimate the fire until it was too late.13

### **Designing for Resilience**

To prevent such collapses, organizations must prioritize "improvisation" and "bricolage"—the creative use of materials at hand.13 Resilience is found not in more rigid planning, but in a more flexible culture that values "heedful interrelating" and the ability to drop habitual "tools" when they no longer make sense in a new environment.13

## **Verification for CCP Application: The Enacted Research Environment**

The application of sensemaking theory to research practice (the CCP framework) confirms three fundamental claims regarding the nature of findings and the role of the research trigger.

### **The Trigger as the Enacted Environment**

The research directive or "trigger" functions as the enacted environment; it does not merely direct the search but constitutes the very conditions under which signal-level findings become visible.8 In the Weickian sense, the trigger is the "frame" that dictates which "cues" from the overwhelming flow of data are bracketed as meaningful.9 Without this enacted framework, the researcher is essentially lost in a "streaming flow" with no mechanism to "chop out" moments for analysis.12 The trigger "creates the conditions under which the answer becomes visible" by providing the initial set of expectations that guide the extraction of cues.7

### **Retrospective Uninterpretability**

Research conducted without an enacted trigger produces data that is retrospectively uninterpretable as signal. This is because no coherent environment was constructed to give the data meaning at the time of collection.15 In sensemaking theory, meaning is "not out there in the world" to be found; it is construed by the sensemaker.7 Without a trigger-frame, the "cues" extracted are random and cannot be linked to a larger story. Consequently, the resulting document becomes a "cacophony" rather than a narrative, failing the "plausibility" test required for action.8

### **Plausibility over Accuracy in Findings Length**

The property of "plausibility over accuracy" explains why a precisely framed 240-word finding produces more reliable downstream influence than a comprehensive 4000-word document. Plausibility, not completeness, determines whether a finding becomes actionable.13

* **The 4000-word document**: This represents an obsession with "accuracy" and "completeness".2 By attempting to include every detail, it often dilutes the central story and fails to provide a clear "springboard into action".2 In an equivocal world, such documents are often ignored because they are too complex to be integrated into the ongoing flow of organizational sensemaking.2  
* **The 240-word finding**: This prioritizes "plausibility and sufficiency".11 It answers the critical organizational question: "What is the story?".13 By providing a coherent, believable account, it enables the "continuation of action" which is the primary utility of organizational sensemaking.13 Its brevity and clarity allow it to be easily retained, diffused, and replicated throughout the organization, making it a more effective "sensegiving" tool than a longer, more "accurate" document.13

| Finding Format | Cognitive Focus | Organizational Impact |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **4000-word Document** | Accuracy and Completeness.2 | Paralysis; obscured "story"; low actionability.16 |
| **240-word Finding** | Plausibility and Sufficiency.11 | Springboard into action; high diffusion; momentum.13 |

## **Second and Third-Order Insights into Organizational Meaning**

The synthesis of Weick, Dervin, and Klein yields several deeper insights into how organizations navigate the "precipice of chaos".25

### **Causal Relationship: The Action-Momentum Cycle**

There is a direct causal relationship between action and the stabilization of reality. In sensemaking, "organizational movement" is the goal.13 Any action that provides a "story" allows movement to continue, and that movement then generates new data that can be used to refine the story.12 This suggests that the "correctness" of an initial finding is less important than its ability to generate momentum. Action is the primary mechanism for "manipulating" the environment until it yields a signal that can be retrospectively understood.8

### **The Ripple Effect of Sense-Giving**

"Sensegiving"—the attempt to influence how others understand an issue—creates a ripple effect throughout the organization.23 When high-power individuals or well-framed research findings provide a plausible "label" for a confusing event, that label becomes a cue for others.13 This "mise-en-sens" (placing into sense) can stabilize a team in crisis or, if the label is misaligned with the team's identity, it can accelerate a cosmology episode.3

### **Implicit Logic of the "Gappy" Environment**

Dervin’s "gappy" reality and Weick’s "equivocality" suggest that the environment is naturally in a state of flux. The third-order implication is that "stability is an accomplishment," not a natural state.12 Therefore, the function of a research framework is not to "map" a stable territory, but to provide a series of "provisional maps" that are abandoned as soon as they are no longer credible.1 The "map" (the finding) is a tool for the "journey" (the organizing process), and its value lies entirely in its ability to bridge the current "gap" in understanding.24

## **Conclusion: The Findings as Enacted Realities**

This research confirms that sensemaking is the fundamental cognitive process of enactment and retrospective meaning construction. The core claim is verified: sensemaking is not the discovery of meaning, but the creation of it through action and interpretation.8 For high-stakes research and organizational leadership, this implies that the "finding" is not a representation of a fact but a "plausible image" that rationalizes action.2

The "trigger" is indeed the constituent environment, and the framing of inquiry determines what data is available to be found.7 Identity, plausibility over accuracy, and ongoing action are the constitutive elements of a finding. A precisely framed 240-word finding is the optimal unit for this process, as it provides the necessary "plausibility" to maintain organizational momentum without the paralyzing "cacophony" of unnecessary completeness.13 Organizations succeed not by being the most accurate, but by being the most effective at making sense of their own inventions in an inherently unknowable world.7
